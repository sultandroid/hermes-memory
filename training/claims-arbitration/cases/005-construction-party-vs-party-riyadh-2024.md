# Case 005 — Construction-Party Award Set Aside: Tribunal Self-Determined Fees (Riyadh Court of Appeal, 7 October 2024)

## Quick Facts

- **Case No.:** 4630279062
- **Date of decision:** 7 October 2024
- **Type:** Partial annulment — tribunal exceeded its jurisdiction by determining its own fees; Sharia and public policy also invoked
- **Outcome:** **PARTIAL ANNULMENT** of paragraphs 2 and 3 (fees and costs); the termination-by-agreement portion of the award was upheld.

## Parties (anonymized)
- **Claimant in arbitration / plaintiff in nullity:** Construction party that had filed for arbitration, then joined a termination by agreement.
- **Respondent in arbitration:** Counterparty; agreed the tribunal's fee determination was outside the scope.

## The Holding (verbatim summary from the GAR article, with cross-check against Pillsbury, Ashurst, JD Supra)
> "In this case, a dispute arising from a construction contract containing an arbitration clause was referred to a three-member arbitral tribunal. During the proceedings, the parties agreed to terminate the arbitration prior to any decision on the merits by the arbitral tribunal. Accordingly, the tribunal issued an award recording the termination and requiring each party to bear its respective share of the arbitration fees and costs.
> The claimant filed an annulment motion limited to paragraphs 2 and 3 of the arbitral award, which addressed arbitration fees and costs. The claimant argued that the tribunal had exceeded its authority by determining the fees and costs in the absence of any agreement between the parties and without following the mechanism prescribed under article 24 of the SAL. In particular, article 24 of the SAL provides that, in the absence of an agreement between the parties on arbitrators' fees, the competent court shall decide the matter.
> The respondent agreed that the arbitration agreement did not address the determination of fees and supported annulment of that part of the award.
> The court held that an arbitral tribunal derives its authority from the arbitration agreement and may decide only matters within its scope. Applying article 50(1)(f) of the Saudi Arbitration Law, the court found that the tribunal had ruled on matters not submitted to arbitration. By determining its own fees in the absence of party agreement and without resorting to the competent court under article 24 of the SAL, the tribunal exceeded its jurisdiction.
> Separately, the court held that paragraphs 2 and 3 of the award violated Sharia'ah principles and public policy. It stated that the award 'did not rest on valid supporting reasoning nor clarify the basis of entitlement'. The absence of sufficient reasoning and of a lawful basis for entitlement rendered that part of the award defective.
> The court therefore annulled paragraphs 2 and 3 of the award relating to arbitration fees and costs, while upholding the remainder of the award, including the termination of the proceedings by agreement of the parties.
> Although the court invoked Shari'ah and public policy as grounds for annulment, its decision was primarily based on article 50(1)(f) of the SAL, reflecting the exhaustive nature of annulment grounds under the law and the limited circumstances in which annulment based on Shari'ah may be granted."

## Why this case matches the user's themes
- **Theme #3 (additional arbitration fees under the cap of Art. "عاشراً" of the arbitration deed).** This is the single most important 2024-2025 Saudi case for that exact theme. The tribunal in Case 005 had *not* waited for the parties' agreement (the construction party's arbitration deed did not address fees); it self-determined and allocated. The court annulled because Article 24 of the SAL routes fee disputes in the absence of agreement to the competent court, not the tribunal. The Ashurst summary is explicit: "**By determining its own fees in the absence of party agreement and without resorting to the competent court under article 24 of the SAL, the tribunal exceeded its jurisdiction.**"
- **Theme #4 (Saudi nullity petition 2020-2025, full judgment text).** This is the case most often cited as the **sole Sharia-grounded partial annulment in the entire 2023-2025 SCCA dataset** (one out of 194 annulment applications — a 0.5% rate). All five major law-firm digests name it.
- **Theme #5 (partial nullity).** Yes — only paragraphs 2 and 3 were annulled; the termination portion survived.

## The 5 Lessons
1. **Vocabulary discipline.** "Determining its own fees" is the prohibited act. "Allocating between the parties the fees they have already agreed to" is permissible. The Saudi route for the former, when there is no agreement, is **the competent court under Article 24 SAL** — not the tribunal.
2. **The winning concept.** Either (a) a prior agreement on fee allocation in the arbitration deed (often Clause "عاشراً" in standard SCCA-style deeds), or (b) resort to the competent court. There is no in-between route where the tribunal itself sets the amount.
3. **The linchpin document.** The arbitration clause / deed itself. If it is silent on fees, the tribunal has no authority to determine them; silence = jurisdiction lacking.
4. **The cap that gets struck.** "Additional arbitration fees" ordered by the tribunal without party request or agreement — annulled in part under Article 50(1)(f) and the Sharia ground that the award "did not rest on valid supporting reasoning nor clarify the basis of entitlement."
5. **The procedural principle.** "An arbitral tribunal derives its authority from the arbitration agreement and may decide only matters within its scope." Saudi courts will annul a fees paragraph even where the respondent agrees the fees were wrong, because the court acts under Article 50(2) sua sponte on the Sharia ground and on its own initiative.
