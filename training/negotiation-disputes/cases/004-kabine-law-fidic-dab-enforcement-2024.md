## Summary
Comparative-law analysis of what happens when a party tries to shortcut FIDIC's mandatory DAB stage and proceed straight to arbitration. Cites the Swiss Federal Supreme Court's examination of Clause 20 of FIDIC 1999 and its treatment of partially-issued awards as enforceable, while noting that English and French courts have considered the futility of the DAB process as a factor in refusing to enforce or compel arbitration. The article closes with the uncomfortable conclusion that "uniform interpretation of the nature of the DAB is still not achieved internationally" — the outcome turns on the governing law and the procedural steps actually followed.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "These decisions demonstrate that a uniform interpretation of the nature of the DAB is still not achieved internationally, and the facts of the case, as well as the governing law, are crucial."

## Lesson Extracted
"Skipping" the DAB to get to arbitration faster is a strategic gamble: in some civil-law seats (Switzerland in the case surveyed) the award may still be enforced; in common-law seats (England, France in the case surveyed) the failure to follow the multi-tier steps can be a ground to refuse enforcement. Drafters must assume that the governing-law variable decides the outcome — and must thread the FIDIC multi-tier clause precisely to the seat of arbitration.

## How to use this in a pre-arbitration playbook
Use this when counsel is tempted to bypass the DAB to save 84 days. The "we can skip straight to arbitration" pitch is wrong in roughly half of the major seats. The defensible move is to comply with the multi-tier clause and treat the DAB decision as interim-binding ("comply now, argue later").
