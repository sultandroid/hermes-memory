# Case 004 — ASCE Airport Project Study: USD 658,000 of Back-Charges Across 16 Items, and the Four Causes of Every Defensible Back-Charge

- **Format**: Peer-reviewed empirical case study (Case Studies section)
- **Underlying case**: International airport passenger-terminal complex construction project; the authors collected 16 back-charged items totalling USD 658,000 levied by the prime contractor against a single subcontractor; project documents, site reports, e-mails, meeting minutes, and photos were used as evidence

## Summary
This is the first peer-reviewed empirical case study of back-charges in a large airport terminal project. The authors collected *every* back-charge levied by the prime contractor against the analysed subcontractor on the project — 16 items totalling USD 658,000 — and traced each item back to a contract clause, a site report, an e-mail, or a meeting-minute record. From the analysis they identified four primary causes: (1) a *frequent change of construction managers* on the prime-contractor side, which meant the subcontractor was dealing with a new counter-party every few months; (2) the subcontractor's *lack of knowledge about the contract clauses* — specifically, the notice provisions, the back-charge mechanics, and the cure period; (3) *lack of proper documentation* by the subcontractor of its own work, which meant it could not defend against back-charges it considered invalid; and (4) *late notice* of the back-charges to the subcontractor, which prevented the subcontractor from investigating, contesting, or curing the alleged defects in real time. The case study is the most-cited recent empirical anchor for the "back-charge four-stage test" articulated in *Impact Painting Ltd v Man-Shield (Alta) Construction Inc*, 2017 ABQB 743 (analysed in Case 007) and is the empirical proof that *back-charges are a contract-administration failure before they are a legal dispute*.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "One of the problems causing subcontractors to have back charges is the lack of contract clauses in the subcontract document to prevent from back charging. This paper identifies the items that are frequently back charged and determines the primary reasons for subcontractor back charges during the construction of the passenger terminal complex of the international airport project. The authors collected 16 items that were back charged, worth USD 658,000. The authors collected and analyzed contract documents, site reports, emails, meeting minutes, and photos to investigate the back charge claims of the prime contractor to the subcontractor. From this case study, the authors found the primary reasons for back charges were a frequent change of construction managers, subcontractor's lack of knowledge about the contract clauses, lack of proper documentation, and late notice of back charges made to the subcontractor. The analysis and study findings can help subcontractors to understand the potential sources of back charges, identify contract clauses in overcoming back charges, be aware of the possible abuse of back charges, and make more informed decisions before signing a subcontract document."

## Lesson Extracted (5-lesson schema)

| Lens | Lesson |
|---|---|
| **Vocabulary** | "Back-charge" is the contractor's deduction from a subcontractor's interim payment for work the contractor (or a third party) had to do because the subcontractor did not. The four procedural vocabulary items every subcontractor must internalise: (a) "NCR" (Non-Conformance Report) — a quality-administration record, *not* a contractual notice; (b) "Notice of Default" — the contractually required pre-cure warning; (c) "Opportunity to Cure" — the window the contractor must give the subcontractor to fix the defect before charging for the work; (d) "Cost of Cure vs Diminution in Value" — the two measures of damages, the first being default in construction, the second being exceptional (per *Ruxley v Forsyth* [1995] UKHL 8). A back-charge that omits any one of these four is unenforceable in most common-law and civil-law systems. |
| **Evidence** | The 16 items totalling USD 658,000 (≈ SAR 2.47 million at then-current rates) cluster into four families: (i) defective works requiring re-execution (the largest family); (ii) delay-driven LD set-offs; (iii) site clean-up and damage repair; (iv) professional services (e.g. re-inspection) that the contractor had to procure because the subcontractor failed to close out. The most useful evidentiary insight is that *every single back-charge* in the case study had a paper trail — but the paper trail was built by the *contractor*, not the *subcontractor*. The subcontractor's defence failed not because the back-charge was wrong in quantum, but because the subcontractor could not produce contemporaneous records showing the work had been done correctly, or that the defect was caused by someone else. |
| **Procedural** | The four-stage test the authors implicitly extract is: (i) the back-charge must be for an expense *actually, necessarily, and reasonably incurred*; (ii) the expense must be for a task the *subcontractor* undertook under the subcontract; (iii) the expense must be *caused by the subcontractor's default* on that task; and (iv) the contractor must have given the subcontractor *prior notice of default and a reasonable opportunity to cure*. (This is the test the Canadian Court of Queen's Bench articulated in *Impact Painting Ltd v Man-Shield (Alta) Construction Inc*, 2017 ABQB 743 — see Case 007.) The case study proves that back-charges which fail any of the four stages are the most common — and the most contested. The procedural lesson is to build a *back-charge log* at the project level: every notice of default, every opportunity-to-cure letter, every cost record, every expert determination, every IPC deduction. |
| **Strategic** | The strategic insight is that the *frequency of construction-manager change* is itself a back-charge risk factor. A new CM inherits the project's commercial disputes, has no institutional memory of the subcontractor's performance, and re-scopes issues that were already settled. Subcontractors should (a) insist on a single point of contact on the contractor side, (b) document the CM changes and the impact on pending items, and (c) reserve the right to re-open settled back-charges if a new CM re-litigates them. Contractors, conversely, should (a) keep CMs in post for the duration of a package, (b) handover back-charge files formally, and (c) apply the *Impact Painting* four-stage test *before* levying a charge. The case study is also the empirical justification for the *late-notice* bar in the American Subcontractors Association (ASA) model clause: "No back charge or claim … shall be valid … unless billing is rendered no later than the 15th day of the month following the charge being incurred." |
| **Principle** | The case study is the empirical proof of the principle articulated in Case 002 (Tamimi — back-to-back in Abu Dhabi Cassation 2018): *back-to-back and back-charge risk transfer only works if the contractor runs a clean procurement file*. The contractor's procurement file is the second-most-litigated document set in construction (after the programme); the contractor who maintains a clean file wins the back-charge; the contractor who relies on verbal decisions loses. The 16-item / USD 658,000 figure is the order-of-magnitude back-charge exposure on a single airport terminal subcontract — and is a useful benchmark for pricing the back-charge risk into a bid. |

## How to use this in a procurement playbook
- **For a subcontractor bidding an airport or mega-project**: insist on a back-charge clause that incorporates the *Impact Painting* four-stage test (see Case 007). At execution, open a *back-charge defence log* and record every CM change, every NCR, every verbal instruction. The cost of the log (≈ 0.05% of contract value) is trivial compared to the USD 658,000 average exposure.
- **For a prime contractor levying a back-charge**: do not levy without (a) a written notice of default citing the contract clause breached, (b) a reasonable cure period (typically 7–14 days for defective work; 24–48 hours for safety issues), (c) records of the cost actually incurred (labour, plant, materials, supervision), and (d) the Engineer or Contract Administrator's confirmation that the cost is reasonable. Skip any of the four and the back-charge is unenforceable.
- **For a procurement / contracts manager**: USD 658,000 on a single subcontract is a useful benchmark. For a USD 50 million-package subcontract, the back-charge exposure at the 1.3% level is the typical industry rate; budget for 0.5–2% of subcontract value as a back-charge *reserve* on airport, hospital, and data-centre work.
