# Case 007 — Impact Painting v Man-Shield: The Four-Stage Test for Defensible Back-Charges (Common-Law Anchor for the Procurement File)

## Summary
The IBA article by Kim (Blackrock Expert Services, London) and Yun (Al Tamimi & Co, London) is the leading modern comparative-law treatment of back-charges in common-law jurisdictions (Canada, UK, US). The article's centrepiece is the Court of Queen's Bench of Alberta's 2017 decision in *Impact Painting Ltd v Man-Shield (Alta) Construction Inc*, 2017 ABQB 743 — a CAD 209,000 back-charge counter-claim by the contractor against the painting subcontractor on a retirement-community construction project in Edmonton. The court held that a back-charge is only enforceable if the contractor proves four stages: (1) the back-charge is for an *expense actually, necessarily and reasonably incurred*; (2) the charge is for a task for which the *subcontractor undertook responsibility*; (3) the *general contractor incurred the expense because the subcontractor defaulted* on that responsibility; and (4) the *general contractor gave prior notice of the default and a reasonable opportunity to cure*. The court's articulation of stage 4 is the most useful for procurement: a back-charge without prior notice and a cure window is either disregarded entirely or reduced in quantum. The article extends the four-stage test with a comparative-law survey: the UK position (HGCRA 1996 / LDEDCA 2009 regulated set-off after *Gilbert-Ash*), the US position (ASA model clauses; *Bates v Benedetti* on the "right to cure"), and the FIDIC Yellow Book Cl. 11.4 framework. The article is the doctrinal anchor for Case 004 (ASCE airport case study) and the empirical case study is the proof that the test works.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "It has been a well-sealed principle of law since the middle of the last [19th] century that when a claim is made for the price of goods sold and delivered or work and labour done, the defendant is entitled to set-off or set up against the amount claimed any damages which he has suffered as a result of the plaintiff's breach of the contract under which the goods were sold and delivered or the work and labour were done."

> "A back charge, in which there is a deduction from a subcontractor's payment for a contractor's unexpected costs in relation to the subcontractor's works, can be claimed as a form of set-off or counter-claim. A back charge is basically the claim for unexpected costs: 'it is billings for work performed or costs incurred by one party that, in accordance with the agreement, should have been performed or incurred by the party to whom billed'."

> "In my view, the onus is on the party claiming a back charge to prove that: 1. The back charge is for an expense actually, necessarily and reasonably incurred by the party claiming the back charge. 2. By the terms of the subcontract, or by some other agreement between the parties, the charge is one, or is in relation to some task, for which the subcontractor undertook responsibility. 3. The general contractor incurred the expense because the subcontractor defaulted on the responsibility to which the charge relates. 4. Prior to incurring the charge, the general contractor gave notice to the subcontractor of its default and a reasonable opportunity to cure it." (Impact Painting Ltd v Man-Shield (Alta) Construction Inc, 2017 ABQB 743, at para 28)

> "Meticulous documentation appears to be essential for both contractors and subcontractors. Keeping detailed records will help to support or contend any back charges. For the contractor, it will be imperative to include as much detail as possible when sending a notice of defective work, such as a Non-Conformation Report, an Inspection Report, quality test results and the like. If the subcontractor decides to take remedial action, it will be important to take progress photos for the records. If the subcontractor does not cure the defects, it's essential to keep the invoices and timesheets regarding the back charges separately to provide to the subcontractor/vendor on completion."

> "No back charge or claim of customer for services shall be valid except by an agreement in writing by subcontractor before the work is executed, except in the case of subcontractor's failure to meet any requirement of the subcontract. In such event, customer shall notify subcontractor of such default, in writing, and allow subcontractor reasonable time to correct any deficiency before incurring any costs chargeable to subcontractor. No back charge shall be valid unless billing is rendered no later than the 15th day of the month following the charge being incurred. Furthermore, any payments withheld under a claim of subcontractor default shall be reasonably calculated to recover the anticipated liability and all remaining payment amounts not in dispute shall be promptly paid." (American Subcontractors Association model clause)

## Lesson Extracted (5-lesson schema)

| Lens | Lesson |
|---|---|
| **Vocabulary** | "Set-off" vs "abatement" — the two distinct mechanisms (abatement reduces the contract price where the work is diminished in value; set-off is a counter-claim that can exceed the contract sums). "Cost of cure" vs "diminution in value" — the two measures of damages (per *Ruxley v Forsyth* [1995] UKHL 8, cost of cure is the default unless grossly disproportionate). "Notice of default" — the prior-notice requirement; "Opportunity to cure" — the reasonable time to remedy. "Material on site" — the inspection-and-payment-of-stored-goods concept. "Pay-when-paid" vs "pay-if-paid" — the back-to-back distinction (Case 002). The four-stage *Impact Painting* test is the doctrinal core: (1) actually/necessarily/reasonably incurred; (2) within subcontractor responsibility; (3) caused by subcontractor default; (4) prior notice + cure opportunity. |
| **Evidence** | Three evidential pillars: (a) the *notice of default* must be in writing and must identify the contract clause breached, the location of the defect, the required remedy, and the cure period; (b) the *opportunity to cure* must be reasonable (7–14 days for defective work; 24–48 hours for safety issues); (c) the *cost records* must be segregated (labour, plant, materials, supervision, overhead) and must show the work was caused by *this subcontractor's* default, not by another trade. The *Great Western Drywalls v Roel Construction* 2008 decision is the warning: a contractor cannot allocate clean-up costs pro-rata across subcontractors; the cost must be specific to the subcontractor's responsibility. |
| **Procedural** | The procedural sequence for a defensible back-charge: (i) issue a Non-Conformance Report (NCR) at the time of inspection (not weeks later); (ii) follow up with a written Notice of Default citing the contract clause breached; (iii) allow the cure period; (iv) if the subcontractor does not cure, issue a *back-charge notice* with the cost breakdown; (v) deduct the amount from the *next* IPC, with a separate line item; (vi) provide the back-charge billing by the 15th of the following month (per the ASA model clause). The FIDIC Yellow Book 1999 Cl. 11.4 framework is similar: "If the Contractor fails to remedy any defect or damage within a reasonable time, a date may be fixed by (or on behalf of) the Employer, on or by which the defect or damage is to be remedied. The Contractor shall be given reasonable notice of this date." |
| **Strategic** | The strategic lesson is that the *Impact Painting* four-stage test is now the *common-law standard* across Canada, the UK, and the US, and is the most-cited modern authority by tribunals in the Gulf region when assessing common-law back-charge claims (DIFC, ADGM, QFC). A contractor who follows the four stages wins; a contractor who skips stage 4 (prior notice + cure opportunity) loses, even if stages 1–3 are proven. The strategic move for the contractor is to *always* issue the Notice of Default, even when the subcontractor's default is obvious; the strategic move for the subcontractor is to *document* its own work contemporaneously so that, if a back-charge is levied, the subcontractor can rebut stages 2 (responsibility) and 3 (causation). |
| **Principle** | The principle is the *procedural fairness* of the back-charge mechanism. A back-charge is a *self-help* remedy — the contractor decides unilaterally to deduct money from the subcontractor's IPC. The four-stage test is the common-law check on that self-help remedy: it forces the contractor to (a) prove the cost is real, (b) prove the subcontractor was responsible, (c) prove the subcontractor caused the cost, and (d) prove the subcontractor had notice and an opportunity to fix it. The *Gilbert-Ash* line of authority is the principle's origin: set-off is a common-law right, but it is not unlimited. The HGCRA 1996 / LDEDCA 2009 framework is the UK statutory codification. The ASA model clause is the US industry codification. The FIDIC Cl. 11.4 framework is the international-form codification. The principle is the same: *back-charges are presumptively valid only if procedural fairness is observed*. |

## How to use this in a procurement playbook
- **For a contractor levying a back-charge**: do not deduct from the next IPC until the four *Impact Painting* stages are documented. Use the ASA model clause as the template: "Customer shall notify subcontractor of such default, in writing, and allow subcontractor reasonable time to correct any deficiency before incurring any costs chargeable to subcontractor. No back charge shall be valid unless billing is rendered no later than the 15th day of the month following the charge being incurred."
- **For a subcontractor defending a back-charge**: (a) ask for the written Notice of Default (if there was none, the back-charge fails stage 4); (b) ask for the cost breakdown (if the costs are not segregated, the back-charge fails stage 1); (c) ask for the causation analysis (if the costs are pro-rated across multiple subcontractors, the back-charge fails *Great Western Drywalls v Roel Construction*). Any one of these three challenges is usually enough to reduce the back-charge by 50%+ or to have it set aside entirely.
- **For a procurement / contract manager**: the *Impact Painting* four-stage test is the procurement-side equivalent of the *Bin Belaila* "bona fide" test for bond calls (Case 001). Both are common-law checks on a self-help remedy. Build the four stages into the standard subcontract template; build the cure-period and notice-of-default requirements into the project-procedures manual; and run a monthly back-charge audit to catch failures at stage 4 before they become disputes.
