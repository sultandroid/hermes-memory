# Case 003 — DIT v APD: ICC Arbitration of a Tender Exclusion in a Cameroon Container Terminal Concession

## Quick Facts
- **Theme(s)**: 1 (PQ / bid / tender disputes) and 7 (procurement under international arbitration rules)
- **Year**: Article 30 January 2021; underlying ICC award November 2020
- **Authors**: Jane Jenkins and Elizabeth Forster (Freshfields Bruckhaus Deringer LLP — International Arbitration Group)
- **Publisher / Source**: Wolters Kluwer — Kluwer Arbitration Blog (this post is the public summary of a chapter in the 3rd edition of *International Construction Arbitration*, Kluwer Law, February 2021)
- **URL**: <https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/arbitration-blog/bid-challenges-what-role-can-arbitration-play-in-tender-disputes/>
- **Format**: Law-firm blog post (Kluwer Arbitration Blog) discussing the underlying ICC award and the wider question of whether arbitration is fit for tender disputes
- **Underlying case**: ICC arbitration between Douala International Terminal (DIT) (a subsidiary of APM Terminals and the Bolloré Group) and the Autonomous Port of Douala (APD) under a container-terminal concession for the Port of Douala in Cameroon; the award ordered APD to pay DIT damages and to re-issue an open tender notice including DIT; DIT's shareholders simultaneously filed in the Cameroonian courts to challenge the irregularity of the tender and the legality of the public company set up by APD to operate the terminal in DIT's place

## Summary
This is a procurement and PQ-style dispute heard by an ICC arbitral tribunal rather than by a national court. DIT held the container-terminal concession for the Port of Douala. In January 2018 APD — the public port authority — unilaterally launched a tender for a *replacement* concessionaire, and in doing so excluded DIT from participating. DIT brought an ICC arbitration under the concession agreement, claiming (among other things) that APD's tender procedure breached the contractual obligation to run a fair, competitive, and non-discriminatory tender for any replacement concession. The ICC tribunal agreed with DIT: it ordered APD to pay damages and to *re-issue an open tender notice that included DIT*. The case is a leading recent example of a *tender dispute* being resolved by an arbitral tribunal — not a procurement regulator, not a court, not a WTO review body — and of a *wrongly-excluded bidder* obtaining a substantive remedy that includes being put back into the tender. The Freshfields authors use the case to argue that arbitration is *under-used* in tender disputes, that the WTO Government Procurement Agreement, the UNCITRAL Model Law, and the EU Procurement Directives leave States free to permit arbitration of tender disputes, and that bespoke procedural design (emergency arbitrator, fast-track, joinder of the winning bidder, public hearings) can make arbitration fit for purpose even when public-procurement transparency rules apply.

> "Attempts at redress for tender decisions have been the subject of investment treaty arbitrations. However, demonstrating a qualifying investment has proven challenging where no contract has been awarded."

> "When a bidder is informed that it has not been successful in a tender procedure, its principal concern will be to try and stop the entry into the contract so that the procurement can be rerun. Arbitral procedures can and are designed to allow for expedition. The appointment of emergency arbitrators is contemplated under institutional rules such as those of the ICC and LCIA."

> "Mechanisms for review of public procurement procedures provided for under international sources such as the Government Procurement Agreement (the GPA), UNCITRAL Model Law, and EU Procurement Directives provide for some level of judicial review, but make no express reference to arbitration."

## Lesson Extracted (5-lesson schema)

| Lens | Lesson |
|---|---|
| **Vocabulary** | A "tender dispute" is a dispute about the *procedure* by which a contract is awarded, not about the contract's performance. It is distinct from a procurement *contract* dispute (variations, delays, defects). "PQ" (prequalification) is the upstream filter before bid submission; "bid challenge" is the post-award remedy. The WTO Government Procurement Agreement (GPA), the UNCITRAL Model Law on Public Procurement, and the EU Procurement Directives are the three main international instruments — none of which expressly authorises arbitration. "Emergency arbitrator" is an arbitral institution's expedited interim-relief mechanism, useful when the bidder's main concern is to stop contract entry into force before the award is published. |
| **Evidence** | Three pillars the DIT tribunal implicitly relied on: (a) the concession contract contained an arbitration clause covering "disputes arising under or in connection with" the concession (so the tender-exclusion claim was within the arbitration agreement); (b) contemporaneous records (meeting minutes, e-mails, the original tender notice) showing APD's exclusion of DIT was not a neutral scoring decision but a *targeted* act; (c) the existence of an alternative procurement vehicle (APD's newly created public company) that the tribunal could order unwound. Without (a), the tribunal would have lacked jurisdiction. Without (b), the claim would have collapsed into a contract-interpretation dispute. Without (c), the only remedy would have been damages. |
| **Procedural** | Three procedural steps for the wronged bidder: (i) check the underlying procurement contract (or the concession / framework agreement) for an arbitration clause — the *DIT v APD* outcome turned on this; (ii) make an emergency-arbitrator application *before* the new contract is signed (post-award, damages are the only realistic remedy); (iii) file in parallel in the national courts only if the public-procurement rules require a judicial review route (Cameroon shareholders did so in parallel). For the awarding authority: build the arbitration clause, the disclosure obligations, and the "open tender notice" wording into the original concession to avoid the *DIT* outcome. |
| **Strategic** | The strategic lesson is that *wrongful exclusion from a tender is now arbitratable, not just judicially reviewable*. This shifts the bargaining power at the bid stage. A bidder who is considering submitting a price-loaded bid to win a contract should also consider whether the procurement contract (or upstream concession) contains an arbitration clause — because if it does, the bidder has a much faster and more confidential route to a remedy than the local administrative court. The 2020 UK Green Paper "Transforming Public Procurement" floated a tribunal-based system for "low value claims or challenges to process on an ongoing competition, such as a claim that a specification is discriminatory or that a bidder has been wrongly excluded" — which is the *DIT* fact pattern. For Gulf and Middle East procurements, the strategic lesson is the converse: insist on a contractually clear arbitration clause with a seat outside the awarding state, because the local courts may not be neutral. |
| **Principle** | International public-procurement rules leave *gaps* on remedies that contracting parties can fill. The principle is "private ordering of public-procurement remedies" — parties can, by contract, confer arbitral jurisdiction over a tender dispute that the GPA and the UNCITRAL Model Law leave to the State's administrative courts. The limitation is that the *public-law* aspects of the procurement (transparency, non-discrimination, public-hearing requirements) cannot be displaced by private contract — and so the arbitral tribunal must be designed (open hearings, publication of the award) to satisfy those public-law obligations. The lesson for procurement: a well-drafted concession or framework agreement that includes an arbitration clause + emergency arbitrator + publication-of-award mechanism is the *cleanest* way to convert a *DIT v APD* fact pattern from a 5-year administrative-court fight into a 12–18 month arbitral award with a re-issued tender. |

## How to use this in a procurement playbook
- **For a bidder wrongly excluded from a tender**: before signing anything, check (a) whether the procurement contract has an arbitration clause, (b) whether the exclusion decision is in writing and reasoned (the EU Directives require this; GCC procurement regulations increasingly do), and (c) whether the time for an emergency-arbitrator application is still open. If yes to all three, file the emergency-arbitrator application *and* reserve the right to damages.
- **For an awarding authority drafting a tender**: build in (a) a clearly scored prequalification matrix with non-discriminatory criteria, (b) a written obligation to give reasons for exclusion within X days, and (c) either an arbitration clause with public-hearing carve-outs, or a local administrative-review mechanism that is *at least* as fast as an emergency-arbitrator timetable. The *DIT* outcome is the warning — exclusion without a paper trail is now an arbitral offence, not a regulatory nicety.
- **For a procurement / contract manager**: the *DIT* award was made under the ICC Rules; the institutional rules (ICC, LCIA, HKIAC, DIAC) all have emergency-arbitrator provisions. For a Gulf-region concession, the seat of the arbitration is the highest-leverage drafting decision: Dubai (DIFC), Abu Dhabi (ADGM), or Riyadh (the new Saudi Center for Commercial Arbitration under the 2024 Arbitration Law) are the three realistic options.
