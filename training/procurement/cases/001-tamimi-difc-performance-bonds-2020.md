# Case 001 — DIFC Investments v Dubai Islamic Bank: On-Demand Bonds, the "Lifeblood of Commerce", and Good-Faith Calls

## Summary
This is a procurement-security case from a construction project in the Gate Avenue area of the DIFC, where the contractor procured an on-demand performance guarantee of almost AED 78 million (~US$ 21 million) from Dubai Islamic Bank in favour of the employer (DIFC Investments Limited). The contractor defaulted and DIFC Investments called the bond in January 2020. The bank did not pay, citing a precautionary attachment the contractor had obtained from the Dubai Commercial Court. Justice Shamlan Al Sawalehi (DIFC CFI) ordered payment on an urgent Part 8 basis, holding that (a) documentary credits are "status equivalent to cash" and the "lifeblood of international commerce", (b) the DIFC Courts had exclusive jurisdiction, and (c) the Dubai attachment was not enforceable in the DIFC. The article then contrasts that outcome with a later 2020 unreported DIFC injunction in a Dubai-located construction project, where the Court enjoined the main contractor's call on two on-demand unconditional bonds because the call was made without the contractually-required prior notice of default and on a "fundamentally flawed" assessment of the sums claimed — relying on the Dubai World Tribunal decision in _Bin Belaila General Contracting LLC v Nakheel PJSC & Standard Chartered Bank_ [DWT/APP25/003/2010]. The piece is the leading Tamimi summary of how the DIFC courts police the line between calls that are honoured as "conclusive evidence" of the beneficiary's entitlement and calls that are restrained as unconscionable or in bad faith.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "On-demand instruments such as performance bonds and other forms of documentary credit like guarantees provide essential reassurance to employers against failures in performance or financial default by contractors in engineering and construction projects."

> "The Court of First Instance adopted the established common law position on the enforcement of documentary credits – described previously, in statements adopted by the Judge, H.E. Justice Shamlan Al Sawalehi, as having a 'status equivalent to cash' and being the 'lifeblood of international commerce'…"

> "The purpose of such notice…is to restrict the right to demand under the bond as between the employer and the contractor to circumstances where there is a bona fide claim for payment in respect of which the bond provides security, and to restrict the amount of the demand under the bond to the amount of that bona fide claim for payment. Absent bona fide legal grounds to justify the demand for payment under the bond, the contractor is entitled to restrain the employer from making such demand."

## Lesson Extracted (5-lesson schema)

| Lens | Lesson |
|---|---|
| **Vocabulary** | "On-demand" ≠ "unconditional" by default. A bond labelled "on-demand" is primary; a "default-based" bond is secondary. The DIFC Court polices the substance, not the label — "an on-demand bond imposes a primary obligation on the bondsman to pay, while a default-based bond imposes only a secondary obligation." "Part 8 procedure" is the DIFC's expedited, no-facts-disputed route for liquid claims. "Precautionary attachment" (Dubai) ≠ "worldwide freezing order" (DIFC). |
| **Evidence** | Two pillars of proof on a call: (a) the demand document is in the form stipulated in the bond and supported by the documents listed in the bond, served at the right place by the right method; (b) on the contractor's side, the absence of a "bona fide legal ground" for the call, evidenced by the lack of the contractual notice of default and a fundamentally flawed quantification. Without either pillar the case is unwinnable. |
| **Procedural** | Three procedural steps that decided the DIFC Investments case: (i) serve the contractual notice of default before the call; (ii) issue the Part 8 claim in the DIFC (not the Dubai Courts), relying on the bond's express choice of DIFC law and non-exclusive DIFC jurisdiction; (iii) obtain the judgment within ~30 days of the claim being issued. The Court will treat documentary credits as a "high-liquidity" instrument where any delay "would indeed undermine the commercial purpose of such instruments". |
| **Strategic** | Employers should think twice before calling a bond "to obtain a quick cash return" — DIFC courts will examine whether the call is in good faith and whether the prior contractual machinery (notice, dispute resolution) has been honoured. Subcontractors should pre-emptively build a "bad-faith call" record: contemporaneous records showing the contractor owes the sub more than the sub owes the main, refusal to give notice, and quantification that is exaggerated. These convert a "conclusive evidence" call into an enjoinable one. |
| **Principle** | The "autonomy" of documentary credits is a presumption, not an absolute. The DIFC applies the English common law position (per _Tamil Nadu Electricity Board v St CMS Electric Private Ltd_ [2008] 1 Lloyd's Rep 93): foreign-court injunctions, illegality under a non-governing law, and bad-faith / unconscionable calls are all defences. The lesson for procurement drafting is to (a) choose the seat and governing law of the bond deliberately (DIFC > Dubai onshore for autonomy), (b) build in a clear "no notice, no call" mechanic to give the contractor an arguable defence, and (c) beware the limited but real "good-faith" backstop. |

## How to use this in a procurement playbook
- **For an employer drafting a performance bond call**: copy the language of the bond's demand clause; serve at the address and by the method stipulated; attach every document the bond requires; do not rely on the bond being labelled "on-demand" if the substance shows secondary obligations; and do not skip the contractual notice-of-default step even if you "know" the call is good.
- **For a contractor defending a call**: file urgently in the DIFC (not the Dubai Courts) using Part 8; rely on the bond's choice of DIFC law and the _Bin Belaila_ test (no bona fide ground + no proper notice → restraint); use the _Tamil Nadu_ principle to neutralise any foreign injunction the employer has obtained.
- **For a procurement / contract manager**: the AED 78 million / US$ 21 million figure is the order-of-magnitude bond size for a tower project in the DIFC; the 30-day DIFC timeline is the time-budget for emergency call enforcement; and the "no notice, no call" mechanic is the single highest-value drafting change to negotiate.
