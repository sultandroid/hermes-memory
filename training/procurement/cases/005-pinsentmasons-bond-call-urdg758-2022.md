## Summary
Pinsent Masons' November 2022 Out-Law analysis is the most-cited modern UK-side primer on how to *make* a construction-contract bond call without having it set aside. The article does three things: (1) it draws the substance-over-form distinction between an *on-demand* bond and a *default-based* bond, citing *IIG Capital v Vander Merwe* [2008] EWCA Civ 542 as the leading authority that a single instrument can be a hybrid and the court will look through the label to the substance; (2) it sets out the *strict-compliance* test (followed in *Simic* in New South Wales and in the Hong Kong Court of Appeal's *West Kowloon* decision) and contrasts it with the *in-substance-compliance* test (followed in the English Court of Appeal's 1990 *IE Contractors* decision) — the practical rule being that *strict* compliance is now the safer course; (3) it explains the *ICC Uniform Rules on Demand Guarantees No. 758 (URDG 758)* as the standalone contractual code that overrides national-law interpretive differences, citing the 2020 *Tecnicas Reunidas Saudia v Korea Development Bank* decision in the English Technology and Construction Court (TCC) for the proposition that the URDG 758 is a "stand-alone code and not interpreted by the rules of national law." The article is the procurement-side companion to Case 001 (Tamimi — DIFC bonds, which addressed the *defence* to a call) and provides the *offence* — the procedure by which an employer makes a valid call.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "Bonds can provide an efficient and effective remedy for a party's breach of performance or other obligations under a construction contract due to the common usage of standard form wordings and the long-lasting recognition of bonds as 'the life blood of commerce' and something akin to cash in hand."

> "An on-demand bond imposes a primary obligation on the bondsman to pay, while a default-based bond imposes only a secondary obligation."

> "It is more likely an on-demand bond if the bond in question provides for a 'pay first, argue later' mechanism for payment. The triggering provisions of an on-demand bond often require the bondsman to accept the employer's demand as 'conclusive evidence' of the sum payable."

> "For parties intending to enter into an on-demand bond, the incorporation of the ICC Uniform Rules on Demand Guarantees No.758 (URDG 758) ensures that the terms of the bond are interpreted uniformly across jurisdictions, as it is based on a discrete body of rules, and therefore reduces the potential legal uncertainty that exists in case law and court interpretation."

> "The UK case Meritz Fire & Marine Insurance Co Ltd v Jan de Nil NV and Codralux SA, provided a vivid illustration of the principles. In this case, the bondsman issued advance payment guarantees to an employer as security for the advance payments made to the contractor. The guarantees expressly incorporated the URDG. The contract was subsequently novated to a new contractor without the consent of the bondsman. When the new contractor defaulted, the guarantees were called but the bondsman refused to pay, arguing that it was no longer liable under the guarantees because the original contractor had dissolved. The court did not accept the bondsman's argument and held that it was obliged to pay as the URDG stipulated that payment is to be made against documents without reference to the terms of the underlying contract."

> "In the IE Contractors case, the demand was written in both English and Arabic and the language in the requirement was quite lengthy and vague and the court found that the parties could not have intended strict compliance. In the Simic case, the courts emphasised the position that banks or other sureties should not be expected to investigate the relationship between the contractor and the employer, and therefore the bank did not need to investigate whether the name of the beneficiary employer was entered correctly."

## Lesson Extracted (5-lesson schema)

| Lens | Lesson |
|---|---|
| **Vocabulary** | "On-demand bond" / "default-based bond" — substance, not label, per *IIG Capital v Vander Merwe* (Eng CA 2008). "Primary obligation" = bond is independent of the underlying contract; no proof of breach needed. "Secondary obligation" = bond mirrors the underlying contract; proof of breach and damages needed. "URDG 758" = ICC Uniform Rules on Demand Guarantees, in force 1 July 2010; a standalone contractual code that overrides national-law interpretation (per *Tecnicas Reunidas v KDB*, TCC 2020). "Conclusive evidence" clause = the bond's "pay first, argue later" mechanism. "Time and indulgence clause" = allows the underlying contract to be varied without releasing the bondsman (only relevant for default bonds). "Strict compliance" vs "in-substance compliance" = the two interpretive tests for whether a demand is valid; *Simic* and *West Kowloon* favour strict, *IE Contractors* favours substance. |
| **Evidence** | Three evidential pillars for a valid bond call: (a) the demand is in the form required by the bond (cite the bond's demand clause verbatim); (b) the demand is supported by every document the bond requires (a single missing document is fatal under the *Simic* / *West Kowloon* strict-compliance test); (c) the demand is served at the right place, by the right method, by the right person. The *Meritz v Jan de Nil* case (EWHC 2010) is the proof that a novation of the underlying contract does *not* release the bondsman if the bond incorporates URDG 758 — the "documents" not the "underlying relationship" govern. |
| **Procedural** | Three procedural steps for the employer calling the bond: (i) draft the demand *against the bond's demand clause*, not against the underlying contract — copy the language of the bond, not the language of the construction contract; (ii) check every document the bond requires is attached (a banker's review checklist is the safest tool); (iii) serve the demand at the address and by the method stipulated in the bond (most bonds require *original* wet-ink, couriered to a specific branch). Three procedural steps for the contractor defending the call: (i) check whether the bond incorporates URDG 758 (if yes, the *Tecnicas Reunidas* "standalone code" applies); (ii) check whether the demand complies *strictly* with the bond's requirements (per *Simic* / *West Kowloon*); (iii) file for an injunction in the *seat* of the bond (DIFC for GCC-region bonds, per Case 001) on a *no-notice* basis if there is a contractual pre-condition that has not been satisfied. |
| **Strategic** | The strategic lesson is that *substance beats label*. A bond labelled "guarantee" can be an on-demand bond (per *IIG Capital v Vander Merwe*); a bond labelled "on-demand" can be a default-based bond (per the same case, where the time-and-indulgence clause made the bond secondary). The Pinsent Masons article recommends a "holistic view" — list every clause that points in each direction, balance them, and identify the *dominant* obligation. For Gulf-region bonds, the *Tecnicas Reunidas v KDB* (TCC 2020) decision is the strategic lever: where the bond incorporates URDG 758, the contractor's defence of "underlying contract novation" (as in *Meritz*) fails. The strategic move is to *not* incorporate URDG 758 if the contractor wants the novation defence; conversely, to *insist* on URDG 758 incorporation if the employer wants certainty. |
| **Principle** | The principle is the *autonomy* of the bond (Case 001's core principle) made operational through three procedural rules: (1) the bond is a *standalone* contract (URDG 758 confirms this; *Tecnicas Reunidas v KDB* enforces it; *Meritz v Jan de Nil* illustrates it); (2) the demand is tested for *strict* compliance (the modern trend, per *Simic* and *West Kowloon*); (3) the defence is restricted to *fraud or unconscionability* (Case 001, *Tamil Nadu Electricity Board v St CMS Electric* line of authority). Together, these three rules make the bond a *high-liquidity* instrument — which is the trade-off the procurement manager must price. The article is the practitioner's checklist for the *offence* (how to call), and Case 001 is the practitioner's checklist for the *defence* (how to resist). |

## How to use this in a procurement playbook
- **For an employer drafting a bond call**: copy the language of the bond's demand clause; serve at the address and by the method stipulated; attach every document the bond requires; do not rely on the bond being labelled "on-demand" if the substance shows secondary obligations. Pre-call liaison with the bond's issuer is a useful risk-reduction step (Pinsent Masons recommends it, accepting the small risk of tipping off the contractor).
- **For a contractor defending a bond call**: first move is to check whether the bond incorporates URDG 758; if yes, the *Tecnicas Reunidas v KDB* standalone-code doctrine applies, and the contractor's defence runs on strict-compliance grounds (per *Simic* / *West Kowloon*). File in the seat of the bond (DIFC for GCC-region bonds, per Case 001).
- **For a procurement / contract manager negotiating a bond**: insist on URDG 758 incorporation (employer-side) to lock in uniform interpretation; resist URDG 758 (contractor-side) to keep the novation defence open. Either way, the "conclusive evidence" clause and the "time and indulgence" clause are the two highest-leverage drafting choices.
