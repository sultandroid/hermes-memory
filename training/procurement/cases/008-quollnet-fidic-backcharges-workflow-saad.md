# Case 008 — Quollnet on FIDIC Backcharges: The Six-Clause Gateway, the IPC-Deduction Trap, and the Worked Example

## Summary
This is the most-cited modern practitioner reference for back-charges under FIDIC contracts. The article's central thesis is that *FIDIC does not provide one universal procedure formally titled "Backcharge"*. A "backcharge" under FIDIC is the *commercial result* of one or more of six contractual mechanisms: (1) Engineer's rejection of nonconforming work under Cl. 7.5; (2) remedial work under Cl. 7.6; (3) defects after taking-over under Cl. 11 (with Cl. 11.2 allocating the cost and Cl. 11.4 governing the Employer's options on failure to remedy); (4) Notice to Correct under Cl. 15.1; (5) Employer's Claim under Cl. 2.5 (1999) or Cl. 20.2 (2017); and (6) Engineer/Employer's agreement or determination under Cl. 3.5 (1999) or Cl. 3.7 (2017). The article is the *practitioner workflow* for back-charges: it walks through the contractual gateway analysis, the documentation requirements (NCR vs Engineer's Instruction vs Notice to Correct vs back-charge notice vs final cost-recovery notice), the FIDIC 1999 vs 2017 procedural differences (28-day Notice of Claim, 42-day vs 84-day detailed claim), the Engineer's role (independent agreement or determination, not Employer-driven certification), and the most commercially important question: *can a FIDIC backcharge be deducted from the next IPC?* The answer is: only after the Cl. 2.5 / Cl. 20.2 procedure has been completed, the Engineer has agreed or determined the amount, and the payment-certification mechanism has been followed. Direct deduction is not permitted.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "A FIDIC backcharge is an informal description of corrective or completion cost pursued through actual contractual mechanisms such as remedial work, defect correction, a Notice to Correct, an Employer's Claim, an Engineer's determination, or subcontract recovery. FIDIC does not provide one universal procedure formally titled 'Backcharge.'"

> "The term backcharge is widely used by contractors, subcontractors, commercial managers, and project teams. It normally describes cost that one party seeks to recover after correcting, completing, protecting, testing, or replacing work for which another party is allegedly responsible. FIDIC contracts do not generally rely on a single procedure called a 'Backcharge Notice.' Depending on the FIDIC form, edition, contractual relationship, and project circumstances, the relevant mechanism may instead involve…"

> "Under the unamended 1999 structure, an Employer amount pursued under Sub-Clause 2.5 is subject to the Sub-Clause 3.5 agreement or determination procedure. Amounts properly determined may then be reflected through the payment-certification mechanism. The Employer should not bypass that process merely by instructing the Engineer to remove an unassessed amount from the IPC."

> "Under the 2017 form, an Employer monetary Claim generally proceeds through Clause 20.2 and Sub-Clause 3.7. The amount's effect on an IPC should follow the relevant certification, agreement, determination, and payment provisions."

> "A FIDIC-related corrective-cost claim should be compensatory rather than punitive. It should reflect reasonable, attributable, and supported cost caused by the proven contractual failure."

> "The Main Contractor cannot rely solely on the FIDIC Main Contract's Sub-Clause 7.6 or 15.1. Those provisions govern the Main Contract Parties unless the subcontract validly incorporates an equivalent obligation or remedy."

> "Worked Example 1: Façade sealant installed by the Main Contractor fails the adhesion requirement in the Specification. … The amount should not be deducted merely because the Employer's specialist submitted an invoice. The contractual and valuation stages remain necessary."

## Lesson Extracted (5-lesson schema)

| Lens | Lesson |
|---|---|
| **Vocabulary** | "NCR" (Non-Conformance Report) = quality record, not a contractual notice. "Engineer's Instruction" = a contractual direction under the Contract. "Notice to Correct" (Cl. 15.1) = the formal pre-termination-cure notice. "Employer's Claim" (Cl. 2.5 / 20.2) = the cost-recovery mechanism. "Agreement or Determination" (Cl. 3.5 / 3.7) = the independent Engineer-administered valuation. "IPC deduction" = the payment-certification step that implements the agreed/determined amount. "FIDIC 1999 vs 2017" = the 1999 form has separate Cl. 2.5 (Employer) and Cl. 20.1 (Contractor) procedures; the 2017 form has reciprocal Cl. 20.2. "DAB" (1999) vs "DAAB" (2017) = the standing dispute-adjudication-board distinction. |
| **Evidence** | The five-document sequence the Quollnet author recommends for a defensible FIDIC backcharge: (1) NCR at the time of inspection; (2) Engineer's Instruction if rejection under Cl. 7.5; (3) Notice to Correct under Cl. 15.1 if the failure continues; (4) back-charge notice with cost breakdown; (5) final cost-recovery notice after the Cl. 2.5 / 20.2 procedure and the Cl. 3.5 / 3.7 determination. One document cannot perform all five functions. The "contractual gateway" analysis is the first step: identify the FIDIC form (Red vs Yellow), the edition (1999 vs 2017), the amendments (Particular Conditions and any 2022 errata), and the executed subcontract (FIDIC 2011 Subcontract or bespoke). |
| **Procedural** | The procedural sequence for a defensible FIDIC Employer Claim: (i) issue the NCR / Engineer's Instruction at the time of inspection; (ii) issue the Notice to Correct under Cl. 15.1 with a reasonable cure period; (iii) if the Contractor fails to cure, serve the Cl. 2.5 / 20.2 Notice of Claim within 28 days (under both 1999 and 2017 forms); (iv) submit the fully detailed claim within 42 days (1999) or 84 days (2017); (v) wait for the Cl. 3.5 / 3.7 agreement or determination; (vi) reflect the determined amount in the next IPC. The procedural sequence for a defensible *subcontract* back-charge is the same, but executed under the *subcontract's* machinery, not the Main Contract's. The 2011 FIDIC Conditions of Subcontract for Construction contain their own claim periods, which may be shorter than the Main Contract periods — the Subcontractor must check its own notice period immediately. |
| **Strategic** | The strategic lesson is the *IPC-deduction trap*. The most common FIDIC back-charge mistake is to deduct an estimated corrective cost from the next IPC without following the Cl. 2.5 / 20.2 procedure. The Quollnet author is explicit: "The Employer should not bypass that process merely by instructing the Engineer to remove an unassessed amount from the IPC." A deduction made in breach of Cl. 2.5 / 20.2 is a Cl. 14.7 payment default by the Employer, which under Cl. 16.1 gives the Contractor the right to suspend and under Cl. 16.2(c) the right to terminate (see Case 006). The strategic move is to *always* follow the Cl. 2.5 / 20.2 procedure even when the cost is small — because the procedural default is the *trigger* for the Contractor's suspension/termination remedy, which is far more expensive than the corrective cost. |
| **Principle** | The principle is that *FIDIC back-charges are a contract-administration system, not a self-help remedy*. The Quollnet article's central thesis is that the term "backcharge" is an industry shorthand for a process that involves *at least* six FIDIC clauses. The procurement file must reflect the system: an NCR, an Engineer's Instruction, a Notice to Correct, a Cl. 2.5 / 20.2 claim, a Cl. 3.5 / 3.7 determination, and an IPC deduction — each properly documented, each served within the contractual time bar. A back-charge that skips any step is a payment default by the Employer, with all the consequences under Cl. 14.7, 16.1, and 16.2. The lesson for procurement: the *subcontract* is a separate contract; the *Main Contract*'s back-charge mechanisms do not automatically flow down to the *subcontract*; the Main Contractor must build its own back-charge procedure under the executed subcontract terms. |

## How to use this in a procurement playbook
- **For an Employer levying a FIDIC backcharge**: do not deduct from the next IPC until the Cl. 2.5 (1999) or Cl. 20.2 (2017) procedure has been completed and the Cl. 3.5 / 3.7 Engineer has agreed or determined the amount. Document the sequence (NCR → Instruction → Notice to Correct → Claim → Determination → IPC deduction). The *Worked Example 1* in the article (façade sealant failing the adhesion test) is the template.
- **For a Contractor defending a FIDIC backcharge**: the most powerful challenges are (a) wrong contractual gateway (the Employer confused an NCR with a Notice to Correct); (b) defective claim procedure (the Employer did not serve the 28-day Notice of Claim or the 42/84-day detailed claim); (c) premature IPC deduction (the Employer deducted before the determination). Any one of these converts the back-charge into an Employer payment default, triggering the Cl. 16.1 suspension and Cl. 16.2(c) termination rights.
- **For a Main Contractor levying a backcharge on a Subcontractor**: do not rely on the FIDIC Main Contract's Cl. 7.6 or 15.1. The Main Contract governs the Employer–Main Contractor relationship only. The Main Contractor must build its own back-charge procedure under the executed subcontract (the FIDIC 2011 Conditions of Subcontract for Construction is the default if the executed subcontract is silent). Open a separate back-charge log for each subcontract; the log entries are the evidentiary record that wins or loses the dispute.
