# Case 006 — FIDIC 1999 Clause 16: Contractor's Right to Suspend, Right to Terminate, and the Proportionate-Suspension Trap

## Quick Facts

- **Year**: Article 13 November 2024; commentary is on the FIDIC 1999 Red Book (Clause 16 first published 1999), with references to FIDIC 4th Edition (1987) Clause 69
- **Author**: Victoria Tyson (Partner, Howard Kennedy LLP — Construction & Projects)
- **Publisher / Source**: International Construction Knowledge Hub (Howard Kennedy LLP-hosted platform)

- **Format**: Practitioner commentary on FIDIC Cl. 16, written for procurement and contract-management professionals (not arbitration specialists)
- **Underlying case(s)**: *Antaios Compania Naviera SA v Salen Rederierna AB* [1985] AC 191 (HL) (commercial-construction of fault clauses); *Obrascon Huarte Lain SA v HMG for Gibraltar* [2014] EWHC 1028 (TCC) (FIDIC Red Book; trivial breaches cannot justify termination); *Mvita Construction Co Ltd v Tanzania Harbours Authority* (Tanzania CA) (timeliness of termination); Final Award in ICC Case 6216 (FIDIC dispute-resolution clauses wide enough to cover tort claims); *TSG Building Services PLC v South Anglia Housing Ltd* [2019] (reasonableness clauses and termination-at-convenience); *NI Property Development Co Ltd v NH (International) Caribbean Ltd* (Tobago HC) (overheads on work not executed)

## Summary
This is the most-cited modern UK-side practitioner commentary on FIDIC 1999 Red Book Clause 16 — the Contractor's right to suspend work and to terminate the Contract for the Employer's default. Victoria Tyson walks the reader through the four sub-clauses: 16.1 (Contractor's right to suspend, triggered by the Engineer's failure to certify, the Employer's failure to give financial evidence under Cl. 2.4, or the Employer's failure to pay under Cl. 14.7); 16.2 (Contractor's right to terminate on seven enumerated grounds, six of which are analysed in detail); 16.3 (cessation of work and removal of Contractor's equipment); and 16.4 (payment on termination, including loss of profit and the return of the performance security). The article is the procurement-side mirror image of the FIDIC.org Termination PDF (raw extract 14) and the Cels.global suspension-and-termination piece (raw extract 13) — together they are the three pillars of the FIDIC Cl. 16 / Cl. 15 termination playbook. The case-study value is that Tyson pulls in *Obrascon Huarte Lain* for the principle that a *trivial* breach cannot justify termination; *Mvita Construction* for the principle that a termination right, once accrued, must be exercised *within a reasonable time*; and *NI Property Development* for the principle that "loss of profit or other loss or damage" under Cl. 16.4(c) does *not* include overheads on work not executed.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "The Contractor has the right under the Contract to suspend in only 3 circumstances, and in each of these circumstances the right only comes into effect once the Contractor has given notice to the Employer (N.B. not to the Engineer). The right is then to suspend work or reduce the rate of work."

> "In cases of termination, the courts have construed fault Clauses in a commercial way so as to exclude reliance on trivial breaches. This stems from the often-followed approach of Lord Diplock in Antaios Compania Naviera SA v Salen Rederierna AB. Similarly, Akenhead J. in the English High Court, when considering the FIDIC Red Book, stated 'The parties cannot sensibly have thought (objectively) that a trivial contractual failure in itself could lead to contractual termination.'"

> "A Contractor who does not act proportionately bears the risk of being accused of being in breach of Sub-Clause 15.2 (demonstrating an intention not to continue performance of his obligations under the contract) and facing a termination notice from the Employer."

> "It is very common for a Contractor's notice of Termination to be followed immediately by an Employer's notice of Termination or for the notices to be given the other way round. Sometimes there is a race to terminate. In such circumstances, both parties will be relying on much the same facts as the basis for their termination arguments."

> "Sub-Clause 16.4 (c) requires the Employer to pay the 'loss of profit or other loss, or damage sustained by the Contractor as a result of this termination'. In this it contrasts with a termination under Sub-Clauses 15.5 and 19.6, under which only cost is paid. From an Employer's point of view, a termination under Sub-Clause 15.5 (which is always the Employer's right), or Clause 19.6 (which is possible though unlikely), or of course Sub-Clause 15.2, will be substantially preferable to a Contractor's termination under Clause 16.2."

> "If an Employer realises it is in a position where the Contractor intends to terminate and good grounds exist for a Contractor's termination under Sub-Clause 16.2, the Employer may save himself considerable expense by terminating under Sub-Clause 15.5. This is a practical strategy recognised in the MDB Harmonised Edition, which expressly forbids termination under Sub-Clause 15.5 for this reason, and which also requires an Employer who terminates under Sub-Clause 15.5 to pay the Contractor in accordance with Sub-Clause 16.4 (c) – i.e. for loss of profit or other loss or damage."

## Lesson Extracted (5-lesson schema)

| Lens | Lesson |
|---|---|
| **Vocabulary** | Three triggers for the Contractor's right to *suspend* (Cl. 16.1): the Engineer fails to certify an IPC under Cl. 14.6; the Employer fails to give reasonable evidence of financial arrangements under Cl. 2.4; the Employer fails to pay under Cl. 14.7. Seven grounds for the Contractor to *terminate* (Cl. 16.2): (a) no reasonable evidence within 42 days of a Cl. 2.4 notice; (b) Engineer fails to certify within 56 days; (c) Contractor does not receive IPC amount within 42 days of Cl. 14.7 expiry; (d) Employer substantially fails to perform; (e) Employer breaches Cl. 1.6 (Contract Agreement) or Cl. 1.7 (Assignment); (f) prolonged suspension under Cl. 8.11; (g) Employer bankruptcy / insolvency. "21 days' notice" is the standard suspension notice; "14 days' notice" is the standard termination notice. Sub-clause 16.3 governs *cessation* of work; 16.4 governs *payment on termination* (including the return of the Performance Security — which is the procurement-security link to Case 001). |
| **Evidence** | Three evidentiary pillars for a valid Cl. 16.2 termination: (a) the trigger event (e.g. the IPC non-payment) must be *documented* in writing with dates and amounts; (b) the contractual notice under Cl. 16.1 must have been given, and the 21-day cure period must have expired without the Employer remedying; (c) the termination notice itself must be served in the form required by the Contract (typically by registered post or as specified in the Contract Agreement). The *Obrascon Huarte Lain* point is evidentiary too: trivial breaches will not be accepted by an arbitral tribunal — the Contractor must show the breach is *substantial*, and the evidence must be proportionate to the breach. |
| **Procedural** | Three procedural steps for the Contractor: (i) give the Cl. 16.1 notice *before* the trigger event becomes entrenched (i.e. before the Employer can argue the Contractor is the one in default); (ii) keep a Cl. 20.1 contemporaneous-claims log running in parallel, so that the cost-and-extension-of-time claim is being built from day 1 of the trigger event; (iii) issue the Cl. 16.2 termination notice only after the 21-day cure period has expired, and *not* before all the grounds are documented. For the Employer: the procedural defence is to give the Cl. 2.4 evidence, certify the IPC, or make the payment *before* the 21-day / 42-day clock runs out — once the clock has run, the right to terminate has crystallised. |
| **Strategic** | The strategic lesson is the *race-to-terminate* dynamic. As Tyson notes, "it is very common for a Contractor's notice of Termination to be followed immediately by an Employer's notice of Termination or for the notices to be given the other way round." The strategic move for the Employer, on receiving a Cl. 16.2 termination notice, is to *consider* serving a Cl. 15.5 termination notice first (the Employer's right to terminate for convenience at the Contractor's cost under the 1999 form, or for the loss-of-profit exposure under the MDB Harmonised Edition). The strategic move for the Contractor is to *identify as many grounds as possible* under Cl. 16.2 before serving the termination notice — because the Contractor only needs to win on one, and the more grounds the stronger the negotiating position. The *Mvita Construction* point is strategic too: the termination right, once accrued, must be exercised within a reasonable time — a delayed termination is vulnerable to challenge on good-faith / estoppel grounds. |
| **Principle** | The principle is that *procurement under FIDIC Cl. 16 is a structured remedy, not a free option*. The Contractor must follow the 16.1 → 16.2 sequence, must give the contractual notices, must keep the contemporaneous record, and must act proportionately. A Cl. 16.2 termination that is technically valid but exercised disproportionately is vulnerable to challenge on three grounds: (i) the *Antaios / Obrascon* "trivial breach" doctrine; (ii) the *Mvita Construction* "timeliness" doctrine; (iii) the *TSG Building Services* "reasonableness" doctrine. The Cl. 16 remedy is therefore the procurement-side *ultima ratio* — the lever that converts a payment dispute into a termination dispute, and a termination dispute into a quantum dispute under Cl. 16.4. The lesson for procurement: do not threaten Cl. 16 unless you can follow through; do not invoke Cl. 16 unless the trigger event is documented; do not serve the Cl. 16.2 notice without a Cl. 20.1 claim log running in parallel. |

## How to use this in a procurement playbook
- **For a Contractor facing Employer non-payment**: send the Cl. 16.1 notice (21 days) the moment the IPC is overdue; open the Cl. 20.1 claim log; reserve the right to terminate under Cl. 16.2(c) at the 42-day mark. Do *not* slow the works to "leverage" the Employer — Tyson's proportionate-suspension warning is the reason.
- **For an Employer facing a Cl. 16.1 notice**: either (a) provide the Cl. 2.4 evidence, certify the IPC, or make the payment within 21 days; or (b) if the trigger event is genuine and a Cl. 16.2 termination is now inevitable, consider serving a Cl. 15.5 termination notice *first*, to convert the Contractor's loss-of-profit remedy (under Cl. 16.4(c)) into a cost-only remedy (under Cl. 15.5 + Cl. 16.4(c) under the MDB form, or no profit under the standard 1999 form). The MDB Harmonised Edition is the model for this.
- **For a procurement / contract manager**: the *Obrascon Huarte Lain* "trivial breach" doctrine and the *Mvita Construction* "timeliness" doctrine are the two highest-leverage clauses to know. A Cl. 16 termination that is exercised disproportionately or after a long delay is unenforceable.
