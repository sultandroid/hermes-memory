# Case 002 — Abu Dhabi Cassation No. 151/2014: Back-to-Back Payment Clauses, Contract Abatement under Article 894, and Subcontractor Cost Recovery

## Summary
An employer contracted with a main contractor to build a tower in Abu Dhabi. The main contractor sublet the MEP package to a subcontractor for AED 60 million on a back-to-back basis: "the Main Contractor will pay the Subcontractor's payments within 7 to 14 days from the date of receiving the equivalent payment from the Employer." After the subcontractor mobilised and issued its first interim payment application, the main contractor refused to pay on the back-to-back clause. The employer then ran into financial difficulty and the project was suspended for approximately four years. The subcontractor sued the main contractor in the Abu Dhabi Court of First Instance, seeking termination of the subcontract, payment of AED 6.92 million, and AED 15 million in loss of profit; the subcontractor also tried to join the employer. The Court of First Instance held that the back-to-back clause was enforceable and the subcontractor's payment claim was "pre-mature" because the employer had not yet paid the main contractor. The Court of Appeal reversed on the back-to-back point, holding that the clause "was unenforceable following the termination of the contract" and that under Article 894 of the UAE Civil Transaction Code the subcontractor was entitled to the value of the work done. The Court of Cassation upheld the Appeal's judgment, holding (i) back-to-back clauses are valid and a prerequisite for the subcontractor's claim during the life of the contract, but (ii) once the contract is "abated" under Article 893/894 (impossibility of performance from an external cause beyond the control of both parties), the main contractor must pay the subcontractor the value of work done, even though no equivalent payment has flowed down. The subcontractor's claim for loss of profit failed because the suspension was not the main contractor's fault.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "The Subcontract Agreement included a back-to-back clause, in which the Main Contractor will pay the Subcontractor's payments within (7) to (14) days from the date of receiving the equivalent payment from the Employer."

> "The UAE Court of Cassation recognises back-to-back clauses and consider it a prerequisite for the subcontractor to claim outstanding payments from the main contractor."

> "the relationship between a main contractor and a subcontractor is the same as the relationship between the employer and the main contractor."

> "where the performance of a subcontract agreement becomes impossible due to external reasons beyond both parties' control, either the main contractor and/or the subcontractor may request the termination of the subcontract agreement in accordance with Article 893 of the CTC. Article number (894) further provides that if the construction contract cannot be performed due to an external reason beyond the contractor's control, then the construction contract is abated, and the contractor should be entitled to claim the employer to pay the value of the work done and any expenses paid for the execution of works."

## Lesson Extracted (5-lesson schema)

| Lens | Lesson |
|---|---|
| **Vocabulary** | "Back-to-back clause" = a payment-flow-down provision under which the subcontractor's right to be paid by the main contractor is conditional on the main contractor first being paid by the employer. "Abatement" under UAE Civil Transaction Code (CTC) Articles 893–894 = a contract is treated as discharged where external circumstances beyond either party's control make performance impossible; the contractor is entitled to the value of work done and expenses, but not (usually) loss of profit. "Pay-when-paid" vs "pay-if-paid": in UAE law the distinction is alive — back-to-back is a timing mechanism (pay-when-paid), but it does not eliminate the underlying obligation. |
| **Evidence** | The expert report drove the outcome. The expert established: (a) the project was suspended for ~4 years due to employer financial difficulty (external cause); (b) no new MEP subcontractor had been engaged (so the original subcontract was not actually replaced); (c) AED 6,920,000 was owed; (d) the employer had not paid the main contractor. Without the (a) and (d) findings together, neither party could have triggered Article 894 abatement. The lesson: at first notice of a payment dispute, commission an expert determination of (i) cause, (ii) progress, (iii) outstanding sums, (iv) substitute-subcontractor status. |
| **Procedural** | Three procedural steps on the contractor's side: (i) issue the back-to-back defence in writing at the first payment application, citing the clause; (ii) preserve the right to abatement by filing a counterclaim (or initiating termination) as soon as the project is suspended; (iii) do not engage a replacement subcontractor, because doing so will be treated as implied termination of the original subcontract. Three procedural steps on the subcontractor's side: (i) record the payment application in writing; (ii) make a written demand for payment at each back-to-back trigger date; (iii) if the project is suspended for more than a year, sue for abatement under Article 894 — do not wait for the employer to pay the main contractor. |
| **Strategic** | For the subcontractor, the most important strategic move was to sue both main contractor and employer (employer joinder), even though the Court of First Instance rejected joinder. For the main contractor, the strategic move was to escalate to Cassation to argue the back-to-back clause was still alive. The Cassation holding is the strategic takeaway: back-to-back is a strong first line of defence, but the moment the contract is abated by external impossibility the line is dead. Procurement teams should therefore pair a back-to-back clause with a "force majeure / employer default" carve-out: "The Main Contractor shall pay the Subcontractor within X days of the equivalent payment being received from the Employer, save where the Subcontract is abated or terminated under Article 894 of the UAE CTC or equivalent." |
| **Principle** | Civil-law jurisdictions (UAE, KSA, Qatar) treat the contractor–subcontractor relationship as analogous to the employer–contractor relationship, and Article 894 (or its Saudi equivalent) rebalances the bargain in favour of the contractor (or sub) once performance is externally impossible. Back-to-back clauses in the Gulf are not the unfettered "no-flow, no-pay" mechanism common in English law — they sit underneath a public-policy ceiling. The lesson for procurement: draft the back-to-back as a timing clause, not as a conditional payment clause; include an express abatement override; and price the employer's credit risk separately from the main contractor's performance risk. |

## How to use this in a procurement playbook
- **For a subcontractor bidding into the Gulf**: do not accept a back-to-back clause without a "save where the Subcontract is abated" carve-out. Build a 60–90 day cashflow buffer and an early-warning trigger so you can file for Article 894 (UAE) / Article 182 of the Saudi Civil Transactions Law (KSA) termination as soon as the project is suspended for more than the contractual threshold.
- **For a main contractor drafting the back-to-back**: include the abatement override, but do not rely on the override being read down — pair it with a parent-company guarantee from the employer or a direct-payment / project-account mechanism so cashflow does not collapse if the employer stops paying.
- **For a procurement manager assessing a Gulf bid**: the AED 6.92 million / AED 60 million ratio (~11.5% of subcontract value) is a useful benchmark for "interim payment exposure" if the back-to-back is read down by a court.
