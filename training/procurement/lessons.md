# Procurement — Top Lessons

## From Case 001 (Tamimi — DIFC Performance Bonds) — Bond Forfeiture

1. **Performance bonds are *independent* of the underlying contract.**
   The bond issuer (usually a bank) must pay on demand, without reference to the underlying contract dispute. This is the *autonomy principle* (DIFC law, UAE Federal Law, and the UNCITRAL Model Law all converge here).

2. **The only defence to a call on a performance bond is *fraud* (or similar narrow grounds: duress, unconscionability, improper issuance).**
   The Contractor cannot defend a bond call by saying "we're not actually in default on the contract." The bank pays, then the Contractor sues the Employer for wrongful call.

3. **DIFC and Saudi courts treat performance bonds differently from letters of credit.**
   Performance bonds in GCC are typically *unconditional* (payable on first demand without proof of default). Letters of credit have documentary conditions. **Drafting consequence:** if you want a "conditional" bond, you must expressly say so in the bond text — the default is unconditional.

## From Case 002 (Tamimi — Back-to-Back Clauses in Abu Dhabi Cassation 2018) — Back-to-Back

4. **A back-to-back clause is a contract risk-transfer mechanism, not a contract-substitution.**
   The Contractor remains liable to the Employer for the full scope, even if the Subcontractor fails. The back-to-back clause gives the Contractor a *right of contribution* against the Subcontractor, but does not extinguish the Contractor's primary liability.

5. **Abu Dhabi Court of Cassation 2018: back-to-back clauses are enforceable if (a) the language is clear, (b) the back-to-back chain is traceable, (c) the Subcontractor's liability is not extinguished.**
   The court will not enforce a back-to-back clause that the Contractor drafted to avoid his own obligations.

## From Case 003 (Wolters Kluwer — DIT v APD ICC Arbitration) — PQ / Tender Disputes

6. **Wrongful exclusion from a tender is now arbitratable, not just judicially reviewable.**
   In November 2020, an ICC tribunal in Douala ordered APD to pay DIT damages and to *re-issue an open tender notice including DIT*. The tribunal relied on the arbitration clause in the concession agreement, not on a public-procurement regulator or administrative court. **Consequence:** bidders who are excluded from a tender should check the underlying procurement contract for an arbitration clause *before* signing; an emergency-arbitrator application is the fastest route back into the tender.

7. **The WTO GPA, UNCITRAL Model Law, and EU Procurement Directives do not expressly authorise arbitration of tender disputes — but they do not forbid it either.**
   Contracting parties can, by contract, confer arbitral jurisdiction over a tender dispute that the public-procurement rules leave to the State's administrative courts. The limitation is that *public-law* transparency and non-discrimination obligations cannot be displaced by private contract — so the arbitral procedure must allow for public hearings and publication of awards (cf. ICC's January 2019 default-publication rule).

## From Case 004 (Kisi, Kayastha & Shrestha — ASCE Airport Case Study) — Back-Charges Empirical Proof

8. **The four primary causes of every defensible back-charge are: (a) frequent change of construction managers, (b) subcontractor's lack of knowledge of contract clauses, (c) lack of proper documentation, (d) late notice of back-charges.**
   The ASCE 2020 study of an international airport project found 16 back-charged items totalling USD 658,000 — and the same four root causes across every item. **Procurement consequence:** back-charges are a *contract-administration failure* before they are a legal dispute; the contractor who maintains a clean procurement file wins, the contractor who relies on verbal decisions loses.

9. **USD 658,000 is the benchmark back-charge exposure for a single airport-terminal subcontract.**
   This is ≈ 1.3% of subcontract value for a USD 50 million package. Budget 0.5–2% of subcontract value as a back-charge reserve on airport, hospital, and data-centre work.

## From Case 005 (Pinsent Masons — How to make a construction contract bond call) — Bond Offence

10. **The nature of a bond is a question of substance, not form (per *IIG Capital v Vander Merwe* [2008] EWCA Civ 542).**
    A bond labelled "guarantee" can be on-demand if the clauses impose a *primary* obligation on the bondsman (e.g. "conclusive evidence" of the sum payable); a bond labelled "on-demand" can be default-based if it has a "time and indulgence" clause or requires proof of breach. The court will read the bond holistically.

11. **The modern trend is *strict* compliance for bond demands (per *Simic v NSW Land and Housing Corp* [2015] NSWCA 413 and *West Kowloon Cultural District Authority v AIG Insurance HK Ltd*, HK CA 2020).**
    A demand that omits a required document, or names the wrong beneficiary, is invalid — even if the underlying claim is sound. The earlier "in substance compliance" test from *IE Contractors v Lloyd's Bank* (Eng CA 1990) is now the minority position.

12. **URDG 758 is a standalone contractual code that overrides national-law interpretive differences (per *Tecnicas Reunidas Saudia v Korea Development Bank* [2020] EWHC 968 (TCC)).**
    Where a bond incorporates URDG 758, the bondsman cannot defend a call by reference to the underlying contract (per *Meritz Fire & Marine Insurance v Jan de Nil* [2010] EWHC 3362 (Comm) — the novation defence failed). **Drafting consequence:** employers should insist on URDG 758 incorporation; contractors should resist it.

## From Case 006 (Howard Kennedy / Tyson — FIDIC 1999 Cl. 16) — Suspension and Termination

13. **A FIDIC Cl. 16.1 suspension must be *proportionate* to the breach.**
    A Contractor who slows to a "virtual crawl" over a minimal breach bears the risk of being accused of demonstrating an intention not to continue performance under Cl. 15.2(b), and of losing any extension of time for the period of the "suspension." The English High Court in *Obrascon Huarte Lain SA v HM Attorney General for Gibraltar* [2014] EWHC 1028 (TCC) followed Lord Diplock's *Antaios* approach: "The parties cannot sensibly have thought (objectively) that a trivial contractual failure in itself could lead to contractual termination."

14. **The seven Cl. 16.2 termination grounds are not equal.**
    The *objective* grounds (Engineer fails to certify within 56 days; Employer fails to give Cl. 2.4 evidence within 42 days; IPC amount not received within 42 days of Cl. 14.7 expiry) are easier to evidence than the *subjective* grounds (Employer "substantially fails to perform"). The strategic move is to identify as many grounds as possible before serving the termination notice — the Contractor only needs to win on one.

15. **The Employer can pre-empt a Cl. 16.2 termination by serving a Cl. 15.5 termination first.**
    A Cl. 15.5 termination by the Employer avoids the Cl. 16.4(c) loss-of-profit exposure (under the standard 1999 form). The MDB Harmonised Edition has closed this loophole by expressly requiring the Employer who terminates under Cl. 15.5 to pay loss of profit under Cl. 16.4(c). **Strategic consequence:** the choice of FIDIC edition (standard 1999 vs MDB Harmonised) is the procurement-side decision that controls the cost of a Contractor-initiated termination.

16. **A Cl. 16.2 termination right, once accrued, must be exercised *within a reasonable time* (per *Mvita Construction Co Ltd v Tanzania Harbours Authority*, Tanzania CA).**
    A delayed termination is vulnerable to challenge on good-faith / estoppel grounds. The lesson: do not let the termination right sit on the shelf; serve the Cl. 16.2 notice as soon as the trigger event has crystallised and the cure period has expired.

## From Case 007 (IBA — Kim & Yun — *Impact Painting v Man-Shield*) — Back-Charge Common-Law Test

17. **The four-stage test for a defensible back-charge (per *Impact Painting Ltd v Man-Shield (Alta) Construction Inc*, 2017 ABQB 743, at para 28) is now the common-law standard in Canada, the UK, and the US, and is increasingly cited in DIFC / ADGM / QFC tribunals.**
    The contractor must prove: (1) the expense was *actually, necessarily and reasonably incurred*; (2) the expense was for a task the *subcontractor undertook*; (3) the expense was *caused by the subcontractor's default*; (4) the contractor gave *prior notice of default and a reasonable opportunity to cure*. **Most back-charges fail at stage 4** — the cure-notice is missing or late.

18. **The American Subcontractors Association (ASA) model clause is the most favourable to subcontractors and is the benchmark for "fair notice" in the back-charge context.**
    "No back charge or claim of customer for services shall be valid except by an agreement in writing by subcontractor before the work is executed, except in the case of subcontractor's failure to meet any requirement of the subcontract. In such event, customer shall notify subcontractor of such default, in writing, and allow subcontractor reasonable time to correct any deficiency before incurring any costs chargeable to subcontractor. No back charge shall be valid unless billing is rendered no later than the 15th day of the month following the charge being incurred." **Procurement consequence:** a back-charge issued later than the 15th of the following month is presumptively invalid under the ASA framework.

## From Case 008 (Quollnet — FIDIC Backcharges) — FIDIC Procedural Workflow

19. **FIDIC has no procedure formally titled "Backcharge" — but a "backcharge" is the commercial result of six different FIDIC clauses, used in sequence: Cl. 7.5 (Rejection) → Cl. 7.6 (Remedial Work) → Cl. 11.4 (Failure to Remedy Defects) → Cl. 15.1 (Notice to Correct) → Cl. 2.5 (Employer's Claims, 1999) or Cl. 20.2 (Reciprocal Claims, 2017) → Cl. 3.5 / 3.7 (Engineer's Agreement or Determination).**
    Each step has its own notice and time-bar. The 2017 form is more procedural than the 1999 form: 28 days for the Notice of Claim (same as 1999) but 84 days for the fully detailed claim (vs 42 days under 1999). DAB (1999) vs DAAB (2017) is the dispute-board distinction.

20. **An Employer cannot bypass the Cl. 2.5 / 20.2 procedure by instructing the Engineer to deduct an unassessed amount from the next IPC.**
    A premature IPC deduction is a Cl. 14.7 payment default by the Employer, which under Cl. 16.1 gives the Contractor the right to suspend and under Cl. 16.2(c) the right to terminate. The cost of a premature deduction is therefore the back-charge quantum × the suspension/termination exposure — i.e. the strategic move is to *always* follow the Cl. 2.5 / 20.2 procedure even when the cost is small.

21. **A "back-to-back" subcontract does not automatically transfer the FIDIC Main Contract's back-charge rights to the Main Contractor against the Subcontractor.**
    The Main Contractor cannot rely on the FIDIC Main Contract's Cl. 7.6 or 15.1. The Main Contractor must build its own back-charge procedure under the executed subcontract terms (the FIDIC Conditions of Subcontract for Construction 2011 is the default if the executed subcontract is silent).

## Cross-cutting

22. **The procurement file is the second-most-litigated document set in construction, after the programme.**
    Every procurement decision (PQ, MA, delivery, inspection, back-charge, bond call) creates an evidentiary record. The contractor who maintains a clean procurement file wins disputes; the contractor who relies on verbal decisions loses (Cases 001, 002, 004, 007, 008).

23. **Material Approval (MA) is a one-way street: silence is not approval.**
    Under FIDIC, the Engineer's MA is required for all permanent materials. If the Contractor proceeds without MA, the work is *deemed defective* regardless of actual quality. (Note: this lesson is not yet supported by a dedicated case file — see INDEX.md gap disclosure.)

24. **The four-stage *Impact Painting* test and the *Bin Belaila* "bona fide" test are the two highest-leverage common-law checks on a self-help remedy.**
    *Impact Painting* (back-charges) and *Bin Belaila* (bond calls) are the doctrinal anchors for the proposition that the contractor's right of set-off and the employer's right of bond-call are *not unlimited* — both are subject to procedural-fairness review.
