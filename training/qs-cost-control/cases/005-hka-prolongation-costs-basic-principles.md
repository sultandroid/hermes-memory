## Summary
HKA Quantum expert Craig Enderbury's foundational primer on the two heads of claim that are most often miscalculated in a prolongation claim: (i) preliminaries (on-site / time-related overheads) and (ii) head office / off-site overheads. The article uses a worked example to show the most common pitfall — multiplying the tender preliminaries rate by the days of overrun — and the correct approach: actual cost during the delay period, net of any sums already recovered in dayworks or variations. The same article ties the analysis to the SCL Delay Protocol's "work actually done, time actually taken up or loss and/or expense actually suffered" rule and to the FIDIC 1.1.4.3 "off the Site overhead charges" wording.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "Someone remarks, 'Let's get an extension of time, and then we can recover all our time-related costs', swiftly followed with an enthusiastic reply, 'Sounds like a plan. We can adopt our tender rates for preliminaries items and multiply the rates by the period of over-run'. If this scenario sounds all too familiar, then for some, I daresay the alarm bells have already started ringing."

> "A prolongation claim can be defined as the contractual mechanism for the recovery of additional time-related costs that have been properly incurred due to compensable delay(s) to the completion of the works."

> "Compensation for prolongation should not be paid for anything other than work actually done, time actually taken up or loss and/or expense actually suffered. In other words, the compensation for prolongation caused other than by variation is based on the actual additional cost incurred by the Contractor." (SCL Delay Protocol, quoted by HKA)

> "The project completion date was delayed by 84 days, of which the Contractor is entitled to an extension of time of 74 days. Despite having established entitlement to 74 days extension of time, the Contractor is only entitled to recover prolongation costs for 62 days falling into periods 3 and 4. The actual prolongation costs recovery is therefore £95,572.86 less an abatement of £24,580.00 for costs already recovered in dayworks and variations during the time period claimed."

## Lesson Extracted — five-point distillation for KSA / GCC practice
1. **Preliminaries rate ≠ recoverable prolongation cost.** HKA's worked example is unambiguous: applying the tendered preliminaries rate × overrun days produces a figure that will be rejected. The recoverable figure is *actual additional cost during the delay period*, net of (i) costs already recovered in dayworks or variations during the same period, and (ii) any period where the contractor was concurrently delayed or in culpable delay. KSA cost controllers should adopt the same approach as a default in the cost report.
2. **"Time-related" prelims and "time-unrelated" prelims must be split.** Site cabins, fencing, site management staff, and key plant are time-related (i.e. they cost more if the project runs longer); one-off mobilisation, demolition of temporary works, and most surveying set-up are time-unrelated. Only the time-related slice is recoverable on prolongation. This is the single most-overlooked distinction in KSA preliminaries disputes because the contractor's preliminaries schedule does not flag which lines are time-related.
3. **Head office overheads are a separate head of claim from preliminaries.** HKA cites the FIDIC 1.1.4.3 wording ("off the Site overhead charges") and the SCL Protocol definition ("incidental costs of running the Contractor's business as a whole" — rent, rates, directors' salaries, pension fund contributions, auditors' fees). These are typically recovered on an opportunity-cost basis (Hudson / Emden / Eichleay formulae — see Case 006 for the formula detail).
4. **Compensable ≠ excusable.** HKA's framing — "compensable delay", "excusable delay", "disruption" — is the SCL three-way classification. A force-majeure event (excusable) extends time but does not entitle cost recovery; a contractor-risk event (culpable) does neither. In KSA, this maps directly to CTL Article 97 (extraordinary events / hardship) — a cost controller should label each delay window with one of these three classifications and link the cost recovery to the *compensable* windows only.
5. **Concurrent-delay windows in the KSA claim must be sliced, not averaged.** The worked example: 84 days delay, 74 days EOT, but only 62 days of cost recovery. The "lost" 12 days are concurrent / excusable / non-compensable. The £95,572.86 minus £24,580.00 abatement figure shows the same principle: even within a compensable window, costs recovered elsewhere (in dayworks, in variations) must be netted out, not double-counted. The cost report's "prolongation" tab must therefore show a *time-sliced* analysis, not a single cumulative number.
