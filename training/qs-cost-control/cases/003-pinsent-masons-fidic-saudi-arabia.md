## Summary
Pinsent Masons' definitive practitioner guide to how the 1999 FIDIC suite is amended for Saudi Vision 2030 mega-projects. The guide identifies the structural Saudi edits: (i) removal of the DAB machinery in favour of direct SCCA arbitration, (ii) deletion of FIDIC Sub-Clause 14.8 (Delayed Payment / "financing charges") because of the Sharia prohibition on riba, (iii) overlay of the new Civil Transactions Law (Royal Decree M/191, in force December 2023), (iv) inclusion of the 10-year decennial liability, and (v) Local Content requirements. It is the single most-cited external reference on how FIDIC Sub-Clauses 13 (Variations), 14 (Contract Price and Payment), and 20 (Claims, Disputes and Arbitration) are localised in KSA.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "Most, but not all, Saudi FIDIC-based contracts we have seen in recent years have dispute resolution clauses which remove the dispute adjudication board (DAB) drafting and provide for a final dispute resolution stage of arbitration under rules of the Saudi Center for Commercial Arbitration (SCCA) in Riyadh."

> "Saudi FIDIC contracts often remove the standard wording in sub-clause 14.8 (Delayed Payment) entitling the contractor to receive 'financing charges', otherwise known as late payment interest, in respect of failures by the employer to make timely payments. This is because of the prohibition of 'riba' under Sharia law, which has survived the introduction of the Saudi Civil Transactions Law (CTL) and would likely make any such provision unenforceable in the Kingdom."

> "While any language regarding financing charges is likely to be removed so as not to affect the enforceability of any later issued award, parties still attempt to find ways to include provisions requiring the employer to pay compensation for loss caused by delayed payments, and as our colleagues Nesreen Osman and Mark Raymont have previously commented on this could be permissible under the CTL's provisions."

> "Most, but not all, contracts now contain requirements reflecting the Local Content Law, as administered by the Local Content and Government Procurement Authority."

## Lesson Extracted — five-point distillation for KSA / GCC practice
1. **DAB ≠ SCCA — most Saudi FIDIC contracts skip the DAB entirely.** Removing the DAB means Sub-Clause 20.4-20.8 (DAB referral, NOD, arbitration) collapses into a direct arbitration path under SCCA Rules. The Pinsent observation that this is "most, but not all" is critical: any incoming contractor must read the Particular Conditions to see whether a DAB is in or out, because the answer determines whether the engineer has any binding interim determination power (Sub-Clause 3.5) before arbitration.
2. **Sub-Clause 14.8 is the most operationally important deletion.** "Financing charges" / late-payment interest is *not* enforceable in KSA under Sharia. A contractor that prices its bid assuming 14.8 interest recovery will be unable to recover its true cost of capital in any KSA delay. The CTL workaround is to claim *compensation for loss* (general damages) — but this requires proof of actual loss, not the contract rate, and is therefore harder to recover than the SAMA + 3% formula that would have applied under unamended 14.8.
3. **Decennial liability (10 years) for buildings is a hard KSA overlay.** Pinsent flags the 10-year designer/engineer/contractor joint liability for total/partial demolition or hidden defects threatening durability or safety. For the cost-control function, this means any preliminaries/overheads claim must keep an unbilled retention buffer of roughly 5% of the contract value to cover the decennial tail — and the post-completion cost report must extend for a full decade, not the standard 12-month DNP.
4. **The Local Content score is a pricing constraint, not a contract term.** Saudi FIDIC contracts are increasingly bundling a "local content" score (calculated on local labour, goods, services, material, equipment, assets and technology) and "national product procurement requirement" (price preference to Saudi products). The cost report must track the local-content KPI alongside the financial KPI; failure to meet the score can be a contractual breach that triggers retention-style deductions even when the works are physically complete.
5. **The CTL is now the statutory floor, not a layer above the contract.** Pinsent confirms the CTL applies *retroactively* to pre-December 2023 contracts (with two narrow exceptions). For a contract administrator preparing a final account in 2025/26, this means the good-faith duty (Article 95), hardship doctrine (Article 97), and statutory EOT route (Article 471(3)) operate even where the FIDIC Sub-Clauses 8.4/13/20 are silent or deleted. Cost-control documentation must record compliance with the CTL duties, not just with the FIDIC Sub-Clauses.

## How to use this in a quantity-surveying / cost-control playbook
This is the canonical citation for "how Saudi FIDIC differs from standard FIDIC". Use it as the precondition check before pricing a variation under Sub-Clause 13.3 (Variation Procedure) — because if 14.8 is missing, the corresponding cost-of-capital claim must be re-routed through CTL Article 95/97, not through the contract.
