## Summary
Aceris Law's reference article on the two interlocking heads of claim — *head office overheads* (unabsorbed) and *lost profit* (opportunity cost) — that contractors add to the end of a prolongation claim. The article sets out the SCL Delay Protocol's two-step burden (must show the contractor failed to recover overheads *and* had other revenue-earning work available that it would have secured), distinguishes dedicated vs unabsorbed overheads, and maps the three classical formulae — Hudson, Emden and Eichleay — used to calculate the "daily" HOOH rate. FIDIC 1.1.4.3 ("off the Site overhead charges") and FIDIC 52.3 ("Contractor's general overhead costs") are cited as the contractual hooks.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "Head office overheads are generally recoverable as a 'foreseeable loss' resulting from prolongation unless the specific contract in question, or the applicable law, provides otherwise. It shall be noted that the term used in the SCL Delay Protocol, 'head office overheads', is not necessarily used in all construction contracts. For instance, standard FIDIC Forms of contract use the term 'off the Site overhead charges' (FIDIC–4th, Clause 1.1(g)(i), FIDIC–1999, Clause 1.1.4.3) and 'Contractor's general overhead costs' (FIDIC–4th, Clause 52.3)."

> "In general, head office overheads may be divided into two categories: 'dedicated overheads' – costs which can be attributed to specific Employer delay; and 'unabsorbed overheads' – costs which are incurred by the Contractor regardless of the volume of work, such as for instance, costs of rent and some salaries."

> "Under most standard forms of construction contracts, lost profits are typically not recoverable. Instead, Contractors typically frame their claim for lost profits as a claim for damages for breach of contract. An appropriate rate of profit is often taken from a Contractor's audited accounts for the previous three financial years."

> "In practice, the Contractor is frequently not able to demonstrate through its records its head office overheads and profit, or it is simply not possible to quantify the unabsorbed overheads and lost profit. In these cases, the Contractor may use one of the three commonly-used formulas to calculate its losses."

## Lesson Extracted — five-point distillation for KSA / GCC practice
1. **The two-category split is the first audit point.** Aceris's "dedicated vs unabsorbed" distinction is more useful than the Hudson/Emden/Eichleay formula choice. A KSA cost controller preparing an overhead claim should *first* allocate the head-office cost pool into "dedicated" (traceable to the delayed project — e.g. a project director whose time is 100% charged to the delayed project) and "unabsorbed" (general rent, head-office staff who could be redeployed). Only the unabsorbed slice is opportunity-cost recoverable; the dedicated slice is actual cost.
2. **The FIDIC contractual hook changes the label, not the principle.** Aceris flags that FIDIC uses "off the Site overhead charges" (1.1.4.3) and "Contractor's general overhead costs" (52.3) where the SCL Protocol uses "head office overheads". In KSA, where the FIDIC Sub-Clause 14.8 "financing charges" is removed (Case 003) the cost-of-capital element of an HOOH claim must be re-characterised as a CTL Article 95 (good faith) or Article 97 (hardship) claim, not a Sub-Clause 14.8 claim.
3. **Lost profit is a damages claim, not a contractual claim.** Aceris is explicit: "Under most standard forms of construction contracts, lost profits are typically not recoverable. Instead, Contractors typically frame their claim for lost profits as a claim for damages for breach of contract." In KSA this means lost profit sits under the CTL tort/contractual liability framework, not under the FIDIC Sub-Clauses — and the contractor must show (i) breach, (ii) causation, (iii) loss. The "three-year audited accounts" rate is the conventional valuation.
4. **Formulae are a fallback, not a default.** Hudson, Emden and Eichleay give wildly different daily HOOH rates on the same project (Aceris acknowledges this indirectly). The SCL Protocol prefers *actual cost* over formulae. KSA tribunals will accept a formula only if the contractor has first shown that the actual cost cannot be demonstrated — the cost controller should therefore keep the *primary* actual-cost calculation and the *secondary* formula calculation both available, but lead with the actual cost.
5. **The "opportunity-cost" burden is the hardest to discharge.** Aceris quotes the Singapore text: market conditions must be "sufficiently favourable at the relevant point in time" for the contractor to have a real prospect of earning the lost overhead/profit elsewhere. In KSA, where construction demand has been near-record high (Vision 2030) since 2017, the opportunity-cost burden is *easier* to discharge than in a recessionary market — but the contractor still needs documentary evidence (tender pipeline, declined opportunities, headcount) to win.

## How to use this in a quantity-surveying / cost-control playbook
This is the canonical reference for any head-office overhead / lost-profit claim. Use the dedicated-vs-unabsorbed split as the audit framework; use the SCL Protocol's "market was favourable" test as the burden-of-proof checklist; and pair with HKA (Case 005) for the time-slicing methodology.
