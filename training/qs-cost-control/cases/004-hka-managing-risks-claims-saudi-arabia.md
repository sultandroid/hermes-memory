## Summary
HKA's data-led analysis of claims and disputes in the Saudi construction market, drawing on the firm's CRUX research programme. The article quantifies the GCC and Saudi pattern: Middle East projects overall claimed EOT of 80.9% of planned schedule vs a 66.5% global average, and Saudi projects alone claimed 97.2% (i.e. programmes nearly doubled by claimed delays). The cost of claims in Saudi Arabia was *lower* (27.9% of budgeted CapEx vs 32.9% rest-of-world), but the cashflow/payment issue was 31.6% (vs the global norm) and late/restricted site access affected 30.8% — directly feeding interim-valuation disputes, variation pricing disputes, and preliminaries disputes. The article then sets out a four-part contractor framework: claim procedures, programme management, delay analysis, and concurrent delay.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "The extra time sought by contractors almost doubled programmes (stretching to 97.2% of the average schedule) on the 133 projects analysed. The cost of claims, by contrast, tended to be lower at 27.9% of budgeted CapEx."

> "Late or restricted access to sites or workfaces – which triggered more claims in the Middle East than other regions – was even more prevalent in Saudi Arabia, disrupting 30.8% of projects. Cashflow and payment disputes (31.6%) caused bigger problems in the Kingdom, while late approvals were also commonplace (30.1%)."

> "Even when their record keeping is sound, contractors sabotage their claims by failing in their duty to give the notice specified in the contract. Under FIDIC 1999, for example, failure to do so within 28 days forfeits entitlement to a time extension and additional payment as the employer is discharged of all liability."

> "There is an increasing trend for employers in Saudi Arabia to include punitive clauses expressly extinguishing any entitlement to an extension of time or financial compensation for periods of concurrent delay."

## Lesson Extracted — five-point distillation for KSA / GCC practice
1. **The 28-day FIDIC notice is the single biggest entitlement-killer in KSA.** HKA's framing — "failure to do so within 28 days forfeits entitlement" — is more aggressive than the FIDIC text itself (which actually requires *notice within 28 days* of awareness of the event, with fully detailed particulars within a further 42 days under Sub-Clause 20.1). In Saudi practice the courts and SCCA tribunals have generally read this strictly, meaning the 28-day clock is the most important document a cost controller can produce.
2. **Cashflow / payment disputes are the #1 KSA-specific claim driver.** 31.6% of Saudi CRUX projects suffered cashflow/payment disputes — the highest single category. This is the empirical justification for tying every interim valuation directly to a contemporaneous evidence file: "value of work done", "value of work not properly executed" (RICS Case 001), and "approved variation priced under Sub-Clause 12.3" must each be a separate workstream, not bundled into a single monthly statement.
3. **Concurrent-delay clauses in KSA are getting harsher.** HKA flags the trend for Saudi employers to include clauses that extinguish EOT *and* cost entitlement for any period of concurrent delay — meaning the contractor is "damages and pay" during the period when the employer is also in delay. From a cost-control perspective, this shifts the burden of proof: a delay analysis must now be *concurrent-aware* (Time Impact Analysis with windows, not Impacted As-Planned) to disentangle employer-only and contractor-only windows.
4. **Programme checks are part of cost control, not separate.** HKA's "Programme Checks" table (logic, leads, lags, hard constraints, high float, negative float, CPLI, BEI) is the most actionable list in the article. The point is that a baseline programme that fails these checks will distort every downstream cost report — variation pricing, preliminaries allocation, and final account reconciliation are all compromised by a weak baseline. This is the Saudi-specific operational application of the RICS §3.2 (link the last formal cost report to the final account) principle from Case 002.
5. **"Punitive clauses" are the new normal in SCCA arbitration.** HKA's wording is careful: "an increasing trend" — meaning the contractor must price this risk *at bid*. The cost-control response is to (i) build a 5-8% contingency into the preliminaries for concurrent-delay exposure, (ii) flag the clause in the tender query, and (iii) reserve a documented record of every concurrent period (a Time Slice Window analysis) so that the contractor is not the one carrying the burden of disentangling the windows post-hoc.

## How to use this in a quantity-surveying / cost-control playbook
This is the canonical "data-led" reference for any quantum expert report. Use the CRUX statistics (97.2% EOT in KSA, 31.6% cashflow disputes) as the empirical anchor when arguing (a) the reasonableness of the contractor's extension of time, (b) the cost of capital when financing charges are unavailable (Case 003), and (c) the contractor's duty to mitigate in a punitive-clause environment.
