## Summary
HKA's definitive current guide to litigating construction disputes in Saudi Arabia under the post-December-2023 Civil Transactions Law (CTL) regime. The article is the most current and most-cited practitioner reference for (i) jurisdictional split between the Board of Grievances and the Commercial Courts, (ii) the SCCA's 2025 caseload (47.3% construction), (iii) the new CTL Articles 95, 97, 179, 470 and 471(3), (iv) the retroactive application of the CTL to pre-2023 contracts, and (v) the new statutory EOT and penalty regime. The article cites Al Tamimi (the KSA law firm) directly on the hardship doctrine and Burford Capital on how the CTL is reshaping risk allocation. From a quantity-surveying / cost-control perspective, the article reframes every IPC, final account, variation, and preliminaries dispute as potentially CTL-driven rather than contract-driven.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "Saudi Arabia sits at the centre of one of the world's most active construction markets. The Kingdom accounts for approximately 39 percent of the total MENA project pipeline by value, and construction and engineering disputes already account for approximately 47.3 percent of the 2025 caseload at the Saudi Center for Commercial Arbitration (SCCA), the largest share of any sector."

> "HKA's Eighth Annual CRUX Insight Report, analysing over 2,200 projects in 114 countries worth $2.43 trillion, found that time extensions sought by contractors globally amounted to 65.8 percent of planned schedules. The Middle East has historically exceeded this. HKA's Seventh Report recorded 80.9 percent across 480 projects in the region and 97.2 percent across the 133 Saudi projects within that dataset."

> "Arbitration is available for government contracts, but only with the Minister of Finance's approval and a contract value exceeding SAR 100 million — a threshold the Minister of Finance may adjust. Below that threshold, which captures much of the mid-value government infrastructure work, litigation before the Board of Grievances will usually be the principal formal route."

> "The distinction between a government ministry contract and a contract with a state-owned company or Public Investment Fund (PIF) subsidiary is equally critical. Many PIF subsidiaries and state-owned companies contract as commercial bodies rather than as government authorities, in which case disputes will usually fall before the Commercial Courts, not the Board of Grievances."

> "Article 95 — Good Faith. Parties are under a statutory obligation to perform their contracts in good faith. This matters wherever one party seeks to enforce a strict contractual provision (a notice time-bar or a penalty clause, for example) in circumstances where the other party's awareness of the underlying facts is not in dispute. A court may find that enforcing a technical time-bar conflicts with the statutory duty of good faith. A contractor who tactically withholds notice faces exposure under the same provision."

> "Article 97 — Extraordinary Events / Hardship. This codifies a hardship-type doctrine. If unforeseeable extraordinary events make performance excessively onerous and threaten heavy losses, the affected party may seek renegotiation. If renegotiation fails, the court may reduce the onerous obligation to a reasonable level."

> "Article 470 — Statutory Quantity Notification. Contractors must immediately notify employers when unit-based quantities significantly exceed expectations. This statutory duty exists regardless of what the contract provides. A contractor that fails to give that notice forfeits its right to recover the additional cost of the excess quantities; if the increase is substantial, the employer may instead withdraw from the contract, suspend execution, and pay for the work done."

## Lesson Extracted — five-point distillation for KSA / GCC practice
1. **SCCA construction caseload = 47.3% (2025).** This is the freshest data point and confirms the 2023 Mayer Brown "about half" reading (see Case 005 of the negotiation-disputes index). The cost-control implication is unchanged: half of all SCCA arbitrations are construction disputes, so SCCA arbitrators are highly familiar with FIDIC and the standard heads of claim. Settlement tactics, evidence preparation, and quantum-expert selection should all be tuned accordingly.
2. **CTL Article 470 ("Statutory Quantity Notification") is a new statutory time-bar that operates *outside* the contract.** "Contractors must immediately notify employers when unit-based quantities significantly exceed expectations… A contractor that fails to give that notice forfeits its right to recover the additional cost of the excess quantities." This is the Saudi statutory equivalent of FIDIC Sub-Clause 12.3 (variation procedure) — and it applies *even if* the contract does not. For re-measurement contracts (the FIDIC Red Book default in KSA — see Pinsent Case 003) this is a separate notice clock that the cost controller must run.
3. **CTL Article 95 (good faith) is a two-way street on time-bars.** The article is unusually direct: "A contractor who tactically withholds notice faces exposure under the same provision." This means a contractor's strategic decision to delay serving a 28-day Sub-Clause 20.1 notice in order to build the quantum case is now an *Article 95 risk*, not just a Sub-Clause 20.1 risk. The cost-control discipline is to serve notices as soon as awareness arises, not when the figure is finalised.
4. **CTL Article 97 (hardship) and Article 471(3) (muqawala hardship) operate retroactively.** "The CTL applies retroactively to contracts and events that predate its entry into force" (HKA, citing King & Spalding). For a contractor currently in a pre-2023 contract dispute, the hardship route is now statutory rather than discretionary. From a cost-control perspective, the cost report should retrospectively tag any post-2020 variation / disruption event that meets the "unforeseeable, excessively onerous, heavy loss" test — these are the events that may now be re-opened under Article 97 even if the contract did not allow it.
5. **Jurisdiction is now a *fact-intensive* question, not a contractual one.** Government contract → Board of Grievances (threshold SAR 100 m for arbitration); state-owned company / PIF subsidiary → Commercial Courts (unless the entity contracted as an arm of the State); private sector → Commercial Courts (unless arbitration agreed). The cost-control implication: a contractor's cost report must include a *litigation-cost provision* that varies by forum — Board of Grievances is faster and cheaper; SCCA is international and more expensive; Commercial Courts are slow but precedential. The provision should be visible to the client when signing the contract, not at the dispute stage.

## How to use this in a quantity-surveying / cost-control playbook
This is the canonical post-CTL KSA litigation reference. Use the Article 470 notice clock as a separate running tally in the cost report; use the Article 95 good-faith discipline to justify the contractor's notice timing; use the Article 97 hardship route to re-open pre-2023 events; and use the jurisdictional split to price the litigation cost in the cost report's "risk register" tab.
