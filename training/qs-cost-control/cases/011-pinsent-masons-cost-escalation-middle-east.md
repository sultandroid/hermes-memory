## Summary
Pinsent Masons' analysis of the contractual cost-escalation mechanisms available to contractors in the Middle East (including Saudi Arabia) when commodity prices, shipping costs, and currency volatility spike. The article's central premise is that "most standard construction contracts do not entitle contractors to additional payment for general inflation or commodity price spikes" — i.e. the cost-escalation risk is allocated to the contractor under standard FIDIC and bespoke Middle East forms — but the *hardship doctrine* (now codified in CTL Article 97) is a "potential lifeline" in extreme cases. The article is the cleanest single-source reference on the cost-control-versus-cost-escalation trade-off in KSA contract drafting.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "The law in Middle East jurisdictions offers a potential lifeline through hardship doctrines, which can in extreme cases shift some of that burden back to the employer or adjust the contract to reality. The process of obtaining such relief can be lengthy and is not guaranteed, so it should be complemented by diligent and practical project and contract management strategies."

> "In practice, the risk of cost inflation and commodity price volatility is usually allocated to the contractor under Middle Eastern construction contracts. Most standard construction contracts do not entitle contractors to additional payment for general inflation or commodity price spikes. In fact, they usually explicitly allocate the risk of cost escalation to the contractor – both in international contracts like FIDIC and bespoke Middle East ones."

## Lesson Extracted — five-point distillation for KSA / GCC practice
1. **Cost-escalation risk is allocated to the contractor by default in KSA FIDIC.** Pinsent is unambiguous: "they usually explicitly allocate the risk of cost escalation to the contractor – both in international contracts like FIDIC and bespoke Middle East ones." The cost-control response is to (i) build a cost-escalation contingency into the bid, (ii) keep contemporaneous records of the relevant commodity indices, and (iii) track the CTL Article 97 hardship threshold from day one.
2. **The hardship doctrine is a "potential lifeline" but is "lengthy and not guaranteed".** Pinsent's wording is the right caution: the CTL Article 97 hardship route (HKA Case 009) is real, but it is not a substitute for a properly-priced cost-escalation provision in the contract. The cost controller's discipline is to flag every cost-escalation event against the Article 97 threshold ("unforeseeable, excessively onerous, heavy losses") and to maintain the contemporaneous record needed to invoke the route if the cumulative effect crosses the threshold.
3. **Cost-escalation provisions should be drafted in *both* directions.** A robust cost-escalation clause allows the contractor to recover cost increases *and* allows the employer to share in cost decreases (e.g. when commodity prices fall). This is the "pain/gain" mechanism that converts a lump-sum contract into a hybrid lump-sum/target-cost contract (Case 010). In KSA, the contractor should push for a bilateral cost-escalation provision in the FIDIC Particular Conditions; the employer will often accept this on a Vision 2030 mega-project because the employer wants the contractor to remain solvent.
4. **Commodity index selection matters.** Pinsent does not specify a recommended index, but the cost controller should propose: (i) for steel — MEPS or SteelBenchmarker; (ii) for cement — local Saudi indices (available from Yamama Cement or Saudi Readymix); (iii) for fuel — Saudi Aramco posted prices; (iv) for copper — LME. The selected indices should be named in the Particular Conditions with the source, the index date (typically the date of tender submission), and the adjustment formula.
5. **Diligent project and contract management is the substitute for a cost-escalation clause.** Pinsent's closing line: "it should be complemented by diligent and practical project and contract management strategies." In cost-control terms, this means the cost report must include a "procurement and commodity tracking" tab that records (a) the contractually-allowed indices, (b) the actual indices on each valuation date, (c) the variance, and (d) the cumulative variance as a percentage of the contract value. When the cumulative variance crosses the Article 97 threshold, the contractor has the contemporaneous record to invoke the hardship route.

## How to use this in a quantity-surveying / cost-control playbook
This is the canonical reference on cost-escalation contract drafting in KSA. Use the "cost-escalation risk allocated to contractor" principle to size the bid contingency; use the hardship-doctrine wording to invoke the CTL Article 97 route when the cumulative variance crosses the threshold; and use the bilateral cost-escalation recommendation to propose a pain/gain share in the FIDIC Particular Conditions.
