## Summary
The HSE UK *Lifting Operations* topic page — the practical on-ramp to ACOP L113 — sets out the four mandatory building blocks for every lift on a construction site: **planning, safe system of work, supervision, and thorough examination**. The page is the canonical restatement of *"every lift must be planned by a competent person, appropriately supervised, and executed in a safe manner"* — and it draws the operative boundary between the *user* (the contractor in operational control of the crane) and the *hirer*. Critically, the HSE page says: *"the responsibility for planning, supervising and carrying out lifting operations rests with the user unless these responsibilities are explicitly assumed by the crane hire company under a 'contract lift'."* In a Saudi/GCC context, this is the global best-practice template that any Site Manager's lift-plan submission is benchmarked against; in arbitration, the lift plan (and its revisions) is the **threshold document** without which the contractor cannot rebut a presumption of negligence.

The companion HSE *Confined Spaces* legislation page confirms that the **Confined Spaces Regulations 1997** remain in force and that **Safe Work in Confined Spaces (L101)** is the ACOP — establishing a parallel regime for permit-controlled entries (paralleling Saudi Aramco / SABIC permit-to-work standards). Together, lift + confined-space + hot-work permits form the **three-permit triad** that site-management standards require.

## The 5 Lessons
1. **Vocabulary discipline** — distinguish *"competent person"* (defined under MHSW 1974 / LOLER 1998 as having *"adequate practical and theoretical knowledge and experience"*) from *"appointed person"* (BS 7121 term for the lift planner for *complex* lifts) from *"lift supervisor"* (BS 7121 — the role on the day). HSE fuses these in the *Lifting operations* page; Saudi site practice should mirror these three distinct roles.
2. **The linchpin evidence** — the **appointed-person-signed lift plan**, plus the **thorough-examination record** (LOLER reg. 9) for every piece of lifting equipment that bears load on the day. Combined, these are the threshold documentation any tribunal/regulator will demand. Without them, the contractor is on the back foot on causation *and* on culpability.
3. **The procedural principle** — *"the user is on the hook unless the hire company has contractually assumed responsibility (a 'contract lift')."* Plainer language: a contractor that **hires a crane without a written 'contract lift' is presumptively liable**. The principle generalises: in GCC site practice, "operator + crane-from-supplier" is the default norm — and that is exactly what creates the lift-plan liability.
4. **The strategic lesson** — for **defending** a contractor against a lifting-fatality prosecution, the question is not "did the lift happen" but "where is the *current revision* of the lift plan, who signed it, and does the supervisor's lift-zone log correspond." Train the Site Manager to *(a)* name the appointed person in writing, *(b)* file a new lift plan every time the rigging configuration changes (not just every lift), and *(c)* reconcile the supervisors' lift logs to the lift plan.
5. **The principle with wider application** — *"permit-controlled work has the same anatomy across lift, confined-space, and hot-work: a written risk assessment, a written method of work, and a live permit issued to a named competent person for a defined validity window."* The HSE treats these with the same logic. The GCC site practice must standardise on the *same* framework so that the contractor's lift-permit, confined-space-permit and hot-work-permit files can be cross-presented to a regulator (or to a tribunal as evidence of a procedural-failure case).

## How to use this in a construction-management playbook
This is the **operator-side playbook** for every lift and every permit-controlled entry. Adopt HSE L113 templates as the *de facto* site-management standard in a Saudi/GCC office — they are freely downloadable, have been proven in UK courts for 25+ years, and they raise the contractor's evidentiary position in any subsequent arbitration.
