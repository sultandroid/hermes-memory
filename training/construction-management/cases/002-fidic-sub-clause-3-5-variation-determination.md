## Summary
This article explains the load-bearing change between the 1999 FIDIC Yellow Book and the 2017 second edition on **whether an Engineer's instruction binds the contractor as a Variation**. Under the 1999 Yellow Book, Sub-Clause 3.3 stated that "if an instruction constitutes a Variation then it shall be subject to the Variation procedure set out in Clause 13" — but did not address the case where the **Contractor believes an instruction is a Variation while the Engineer has not stated so**. The 2017 second-edition Sub-Clause 3.5 closes that gap: the Contractor can give a Notice that the instruction constitutes a Variation; if the Engineer does not respond within 7 days (confirming, reversing or varying the instruction), the **Contractor's deemed-acceptance route crystallises the contractual status of the instruction**. The article flags the secondary risk: where there is a *genuine dispute* over whether an instruction is a Variation, the Engineer's determinative power under Sub-Clause 3.5 interacts with the DAB referral under Sub-Clause 21 — a sequence frequently misrouted by contractors who Notice without contemporaneously logging their method-statement implications.

> "Sub-Clause 3.3 of the 1999 Yellow Book states that if an instruction constitutes a Variation then it shall be subject to the Variation procedure set out in Clause 13. ## The 5 Lessons
1. **Vocabulary discipline** — distinguish three terms that contractors routinely conflate: (a) *"instruction"* (a binding direction under SC 3.3 / 3.5 the contractor **must** comply with); (b) *"Variation"* (a contractual change priced under Cl. 13); (c) *"determination"* (the Engineer's interim dispute ruling under SC 3.5 / former SC 3.5). Each triggers a different Cl. 20 Notice and a different cost-recovery basis.
2. **The linchpin evidence** — the **7-day Notice** that triggers SC 3.5 deemed-acceptance. A contemporaneous, date-stamped Notice (ideally with method-statement implications attached) is the single most decisive procedural step; without it, the contractor is back to the SC 20.1 route, where the burden of proving the instruction was a Variation is heavier.
3. **The procedural principle** — *"Compliance + Notice ≠ Waiver."* A contractor who complies with an instruction does **not** waive its right to claim the cost/time consequences. Under the 1999 Yellow (and preserved under 2017 Yellow) the act of compliance is decoupled from the act of Notice. Operational managers must instruct site teams that compliance and Notice are parallel obligations, not alternatives.
4. **The strategic lesson** — for **defending** an EOT/cost claim tied to an instruction, build the contemporaneous record before the **7-day window closes**: (i) date-stamped photographs of the affected workface; (ii) a method-statement narrative of the original sequence and the required revised sequence; (iii) resource and plant-return-impact calculations tied to the as-planned programme. Per GAR documents/evidence (Case 007), this is the *contemporaneous* record the tribunal weighs.
5. **The principle with wider application** — *"Silent acceptance is a feature, not a bug, of modern FIDIC."* SC 3.5 reflects the move in international construction law toward permitting deemed acceptance by silence — provided the notice mechanics are observed precisely. The principle applies to all contractor-issued Notices under Cl. 20 (claims), Cl. 13 (variations), and Cl. 21 (DAB referral). Read it as a *general policy of contractual architecture* in FIDIC, not a one-clause curiosity.
