## Summary
The IBA *CLINT* March 2026 feature on **"Mastering FIDIC in Arbitration: understanding, expertise and the price of ignorance"** articulates a thesis that is the modern practitioner consensus: *"arbitrating a FIDIC contract requires far more than general experience in construction disputes. It demands a nuanced understanding of the FIDIC contractual system, its procedural sequence and the role each actor plays within the structure designed by FIDIC."* The article highlights that FIDIC contracts, although standardised templates, constitute in practice *"a legal and technical ecosystem that requires a full understanding of what is inside each contract."* The article warns that where knowledge and mastery are missing from arbitrators, counsel, or experts, *"the arbitration becomes obscure and unbalanced, the FIDIC principles are misapplied and the party with more strategic skills ends"* with an asymmetric outcome. The article ties this directly to the contractor's site-management discipline: where the contractor's submittal log, instruction log, and subcontractor-coordination log are not maintained to the standard the FIDIC procedural sequence assumes, even a substantively-strong case fails on the procedural-mis-stepping ground. The feature is the canonical March 2026 reaffirmation of the *FIDIC procedural sequence* principle — the same principle that the Fenwick Elliott Sub-Clause 3.5 (Case 002) and the GAR MENA chapter (Case 010) apply to the GCC site.

## The 5 Lessons
1. **Vocabulary discipline** — distinguish the FIDIC "procedural sequence" (the contractually mandated order: notify → instruct → determine → escalate → DAB → arbitral tribunal) from the *project execution sequence* (design → submittal → construct → test → complete → operate). These two sequences run *in parallel* for the entire project; a contractor who tracks only the second and not the first loses the right to claim 14 days after the event crystallises.
2. **The linchpin evidence** — the **submittal register** (every method statement, material, shop-drawing, sample). The submittal register *is* the site-instruction counterpart: every entry is either an instruction received, an instruction responded to, or an instruction awaiting the Engineer's determination. A submittal register that runs without gaps is the contractor's best evidence that the FIDIC procedural sequence was followed.
3. **The procedural principle** — *"FIDIC is procedural, not substantive."* The substantive merits of a method statement or a claim are secondary to whether the procedural sequence was observed. The 7-day Notice (SC 3.5), the 28-day claim (SC 20.1), the DAB referral (SC 21.1) — every step has a timed procedural function. Miss one and the *substantive* right is preserved but the *remedy* is lost. The lesson generalises: the strongest site manager is the one who runs the procedural sequence as a *daily discipline*, not as a once-off dispute reflex.
4. **The strategic lesson for multi-discipline sites** — the **interface matrix** between disciplines (civil / structural / MEP / architectural) follows the same procedural discipline. A method statement for an MEP riser through a structural beam that is submitted late, or without sign-off from the structural engineer-of-record, is a procedural defect — even if the technical content is correct. The article draws directly on the same Gar MENA (Case 010) observation that GCC mega-projects have a structural prevalence of interface-driven claims.
5. **The principle with wider application** — *"FIDIC mastery is the most-failed competence in modern construction arbitration."* The article's strongest claim is that the pervasiveness of sub-mastery (counsel and tribunal alike) tilts outcomes toward the better-resourced, more procedurally-strategic party. For the Site Manager / Project Manager on the ground, the lesson is that the procedural sequence is not optional — and that the contractor's *first* internal investment should be in a submittal register, a Notice log, and a DAB-eligibility tracker, not in expert witnesses.
