## Summary
The GAR Sixth Edition's *MENA regional* chapter is the canonical 2026 synthesis of construction-arbitration practice in Saudi Arabia, the GCC, and the wider MENA zone. The chapter opens by framing the regional dispute environment: foreign direct investment has grown rapidly across MENA construction, with disputes reaching **US$90.4 million in 2021** and the **Gulf projects pipeline in excess of US$2.7 trillion (Saudi Arabia accounting for US$1.5 trillion of this figure)**. The chapter then walks through the typical dispute categories that drive MENA construction arbitration, including *"the engineer's administration of work and specifically the likelihood of late approvals, or incorrect or insufficient instructions"* and *"unjust enrichment or enrichment without cause"*, where *"contractors in construction projects often execute extra work based on instructions from the engineer or the employers to vary the work. Normally, instructions for extra work are regulated under variation clauses."* The chapter then addresses the Saudi seat directly, noting the role of *"Saudi CC and the Islamic legal maxims in Article 720 of the Saudi CC"* in arbitrators' reasoning — a doctrinal anchor that the modern Saudi arbitration practitioner must internalise. The chapter also provides the *unjust enrichment* doctrine in the construction context — a useful framing when the contractor's claim cannot easily be re-characterised as a Variation under Cl. 13.

## The 5 Lessons
1. **Vocabulary discipline** — distinguish (in modern MENA construction arbitration): *"Variation"* (FIDIC-flavoured, contractual right against a price/SC 13 mechanic), *"unjust enrichment / enrichment without cause"* (الغنى بلا سبب / الإثراء بلا سبب — civil-code remedy available where a contractual route is unavailable), and *"imprévision / hardship"* (الأزمة / الظروف الطارئة — equitable-contractual remedy for contract-rebalancing on supervening events). The chapter treats each as a *distinct* substantive basis; counsel may plead them in the alternative.
2. **The linchpin evidence** — **the contemporaneous instruction-and-response log** (with the late-approval / insufficient-instruction theme the chapter flags). The engineer's late approval of a method statement is documented by the submittal-register entry; the engineer's *insufficient instruction* is documented by the contractor's reply or *Request for Clarification* under FIDIC SC 1.9. Both depend on a contemporaneous record — the same framework established in Case 007.
3. **The procedural principle** — *"Article 720 of the Saudi CC / Islamic maxims are the doctrinal anchor for Saudi arbitration, not the foreign-flavoured 'modern' common-law frame."* Counsel for a Saudi-seated arbitration must not import common-law thinking wholesale; the *Saudi CC* and the *Islamic maxims* are the substantive frame. The chapter establishes this as the consensus position — corroborated by the SCCA 3-Year Case Law Study and the SCCA 2026 Country Report.
4. **The strategic lesson for subcontractor coordination** — in a multi-discipline MENA project, **late or missing engineer coordination decisions propagate into subcontractor interface claims**. The contractor cannot rely on the engineer to coordinate the disciplines; the Site Manager must run the *interface matrix* as a primary discipline (alongside the submittal register and the programme) and document late engineer coordination as a contract risk event.
5. **The principle with wider application** — *"MENA construction arbitration is now a US$90 M-per-dispute, US$2.7 T-pipeline sector."* This is the macro-frame within which every site-management decision sits. A contractor's investment in a contemporaneous record is an investment in *project-pipeline-realised-disputes* that have the potential to dwarf any individual project's margin. The lesson generalises: every site-management improvement pays off across a portfolio of projects, not just the one in front of you.
