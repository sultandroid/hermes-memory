## Summary
This Al Tamimi analysis of the **Saudi Civil Transactions Law** (the new civil code enacted by Royal Decree M/191 of 19 June 2023 — a fundamental recodification of Saudi contract law with construction-industry significance) walks through Articles 463–467, which set out the **statutory obligations of contractors** under a *muqawala* contract (the Arabic term for a contract for services / construction contract). The provisions codify the contractor's duty to: (i) supply and safeguard materials (Article 463 — *"contractors shoulder the responsibility for materials, regardless of whether they are supplied by the employer or obtained independently"*), (ii) bear the expense of procuring machinery and tools (Article 464), and (iii) rectify defects within the defect-liability period, with **immediate contract termination** available as a remedy where *"repairing the defect is unfeasible or if the contractor exhibits delay in commencing or completing the works to the extent it cannot recover this delay."* For a contractor defending a snag-list / punch-list dispute at handover, the framework now has a **statutory peg** on which to anchor contractual arguments: the defect-liability concept, the defect rectification criteria, and the threshold for repudiation all sit in primary legislation, not only in the bespoke FIDIC-style contract.

## Direct Quote (verbatim from source)
> "Articles 463 to 467 of the KSA Civil Transactions Law sets out the obligations imposed upon contractors. This section defines the various responsibilities contractors owe to their employers, with specific emphasis on materials, machinery, tools, and contractual commitments. Also, it is important to note that the definition of contracts for service (often referred to as muqawala contracts) is broad and captures service providers, including engineering, project management, supervision, and design."

> "Pursuant to Article 463, contractors shoulder the responsibility for materials, regardless of whether they are supplied by the employer or obtained independently. It goes further to stipulate that the contractor is duty-bound to safeguard these materials, ensuring their use aligns with their intended purpose. This provision underscores the significance of diligent material management, reducing the scope for disputes related to material handling."

> "Article 464, on the other hand, places the onus on the contractor to bear the expenses incurred in procuring the requisite machinery and tools essential for project completion. This mandate ensures a clear delineation of financial responsibilities, minimising ambiguities in financial matters."

> "Immediate contract termination is also a viable option in cases where repairing the defect is unfeasible or if the contractor exhibits delay in commencing or completing the works to the extent it cannot recover this delay. This provision ensures that breaches are addressed judiciously and in a manner that safeguards the interests of all parties involved."

> "The law will have an impact on the construction sector in Saudi Arabia and will provide contractual clarity and a mechanism for dispute resolution with the aim of enhancing consumer interests."

## The 5 Lessons
1. **Vocabulary discipline** — *"muqawala"* (مقاولة) is the Saudi-Arabic statutory term for a contract for services / construction contract (not the same Arabic root as *murabaha* or *ijara*). Under the 2023 Civil Transactions Law, a muqawala expressly captures engineering, project management, supervision, and design — meaning a Saudi Project Management Consultancy or Supervision Consultant is on the same statutory footing as a General Contractor for materials-and-defect purposes. Read this against FIDIC SC 1.1.2.6 "Employer's Personnel" definitions.
2. **The linchpin evidence** — the **chain-of-custody and storage records for materials** (delivery notes, condition-at-arrival photographs, monthly stocktakes, the on-site lay-down-area access log). Article 463 transfers the material-loss risk to the contractor *from the moment of arrival*; without the contemporaneous record, the contractor cannot rebut a claim that defective material was already defective *before* arrival.
3. **The procedural principle** — *"defect rectification is the default; termination is the exceptional remedy."* Article 467's reference to **immediate termination** where the contractor's delay *"cannot recover"* signals a **threshold test**: the contractor must show either (a) the rectification is feasible, (b) the contractor can recover the delay, or (c) the defect does not fall within the contractor's scope. Counsel defending a termination should always plead at least one of (a)–(c).
4. **The strategic lesson for snag-list / punch-list handover** — the **defect-liability statutory period** now sits in primary Saudi legislation. The contractor's handover strategy must address (i) the contractual defect-liability period (typically 12 months under FIDIC Yellow SC 11); (ii) the statutory defect window (cross-check against the project's procedural-status date and Article 467 in conjunction with Article 175 of the implementing regulations); and (iii) the taking-over certificate issued under FIDIC SC 10.1, which *transfers* the care-of-the-works obligation and starts the defect-liability clock.
5. **The principle with wider application** — *"Saudi codified law now has the look-and-feel of a CISG / UNIDROIT-style civil code, not a Sharia-only code."* This is a substantive doctrinal shift from the pre-2023 *fiqh-based* contract doctrine. For international contractors, the 2023 Civil Transactions Law should be read as making **statutory pegs available** that previously had to be threaded through Article 720 of the old *Majallat al-Ahkam al-Adliyyah* (the Ottoman-derived code that the SCCA 3-Year Case Law Study showed was the historical basis for Saudi arbitration reasoning). The lesson generalises: every FIDIC-based dispute now has a *statutory* layer that can be invoked directly in SCCA arbitration.

## How to use this in a construction-management playbook
Anchor every contract-risk memo in a Saudi seat on **Articles 463–467 of the Civil Transactions Law** alongside the FIDIC express terms — particularly where the defect-liability period or the cost-of-rectification is contested. Pair with Case 005 (HSE UK L113) and Case 006 (Saudi HSE tightening) for the *statutory-and-procedural* HSE frame, and with Case 002 (FIDIC SC 3.5) for the *instructional* frame.
