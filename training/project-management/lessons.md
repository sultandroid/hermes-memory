# Project Management — Top Lessons

## From Cases 03 + 04 + 06 (Concurrent Delay) — Malmaison vs De Beers

1. **The *Malmaison* approach is the default in UAE and increasingly applied in Saudi.**
   Under *Malmaison*: an Employer-risk event entitles the Contractor to EOT but not to time-related cost (prolongation), if the Employer-risk event and the Contractor-risk event are concurrent. Under *De Beers* (the older approach): the Employer-risk event is "first in time" and the Contractor gets full EOT + cost.

2. **In Saudi arbitration, the tribunal will pick the approach based on the contract wording.**
   The 1999 FIDIC Cl. 8.4 wording tilts toward *Malmaison*. The 2017 FIDIC wording (with the new advance-warning mechanism) is closer to *De Beers* in spirit. **Drafting consequence:** choose your FIDIC edition deliberately if concurrent delay matters to you.

3. **Concurrent delay is a question of fact, not a question of law.**
   The Contractor must prove (a) the Employer-risk event, (b) the Contractor-risk event, (c) the concurrent period, (d) the cost attributable to the Employer-risk period. A global "concurrent delay" claim without fact-specific analysis is struck.

## From Cases 02 + 05 (Strata + HKA — FIDIC claims in Saudi) — Notice Discipline

4. **The 28-day notice (FIDIC Cl. 20.1) is the #1 procedural defect in Saudi claims.**
   **Operational rule:** on every Employer-risk event, issue a Notice within 14 days, with a placeholder for the full particulars to follow. The Notice preserves entitlement; the particulars can be refined later.

5. **A risk register is a living document, not a tender submission.**
   The risk register that wins the dispute is the one updated monthly, with named owners, current mitigations, and a residual score. The risk register that loses is the one issued at tender and never touched.

## From Case 01 (HKA — Litigating Disputes in Saudi) — Tender Risk Identification

6. **60% of Saudi construction disputes arise from inadequate risk identification at tender.**
   **Drafting consequence:** at the tender stage, push for explicit exclusions and clarifications. An unclear tender scope is a claim seed. The contractor who flags risks in the tender (and prices them) is the contractor who wins the dispute 5 years later.

## From Case 07 (IQRM — QSRA for NEOM The Line) — Mega-Project Risk

7. **Quantitative Schedule Risk Analysis (QSRA) is the new standard for Vision 2030 mega-projects.**
   QSRA runs Monte Carlo simulation on the programme to give a probabilistic completion date (P50, P80, P90). NEOM The Line and Red Sea both required QSRA as a tender submission. **Operational rule:** if you bid Vision 2030 mega-projects, invest in QSRA capability or partner with a specialist.

## From Case 06 (Pinsent Masons — Malmaison / Cyden Homes) — UK vs GCC

8. **UK case law on concurrent delay (*Malmaison*, *Cyden Homes*) is the most-cited authority in GCC arbitration.**
   GCC tribunals apply English common-law principles (where the contract is silent) and increasingly cite UK case law. **Operational rule:** the contractor's delay analyst should be fluent in UK case law, not just FIDIC.

## Cross-cutting

9. **Schedule is the spine of every claim. The contractor who controls the schedule controls the dispute.**
   This is true for EOT, prolongation, disruption, acceleration, and concurrent delay. Invest in programming capability from project day one.

10. **The Programme must be a contract document, not a contractor's working tool.**
    A programme that is not approved by the Engineer / Employer's representative is not enforceable. **Operational rule:** submit the programme for approval within the contractual window (usually 28 days from Commencement).
