## 2–3 sentence summary with direct quote
Pinsent Masons' guide is the practitioner-standard explainer of the two leading English-law concurrent-delay authorities — Henry Boot v Malmaison (1999) and the more recent North Midland Building v Cyden Homes (2018, CoA 2019) — and it draws the line that Saudi drafters are increasingly importing. Malmaison established that "provided one of the concurrent causes of the delay was at the employer's risk, the contractor will receive a full extension of time for the whole period of delay, even though the delay was partly caused by an event for which the contractor was responsible." Cyden Homes then validated an express clause that "disallowed a contractor's claim for an extension of time in cases of concurrent delay" — the basis for the modern Saudi "no EOT for concurrent delay" clause HKA flagged in 2025.

> "Construction contracts often do not address the issue of concurrent delay but the parties can set out an express provision in the contract to deal with this situation. In North Midland Building Ltd v Cyden Homes Ltd [2017] EWHC 2414 (TCC), the Technology and Construction Court (TCC) clarified that the parties to a contract are free to allocate the risk of concurrent delay. In this case, the court upheld a clause that disallowed a contractor's claim for an extension of time in cases of concurrent delay." (Pinsent Masons Out-Law)

> "The Malmaison case set out the rationale that, provided one of the concurrent causes of the delay was at the employer's risk, the contractor will receive a full extension of time for the whole period of delay, even though the delay was partly caused by an event for which the contractor was responsible. The courts went on to adopt an approach which has become known as the Malmaison approach." (Pinsent Masons Out-Law)

## 5-lesson extraction
1. **Default English-law position = Malmaison; Saudi default is increasingly the opposite** — the 1999 Henry Boot v Malmaison Hotel rule (full EOT for the whole concurrent period if any cause is at employer risk) is the common-law baseline; it can be excluded by clear drafting (Cyden Homes, 2018).
2. **Express drafting is decisive in both UK and GCC courts** — the Cyden Homes clause was upheld as "crystal clear" because it addressed the concurrent scenario directly. A vague clause is read down to the Malmaison default; a precise clause is enforced.
3. **The "prevention principle" is a real limit, even on clear drafting** — Cyden Homes rejected the argument that the prevention principle invalidated the no-concurrent-delay clause, but the principle remains live in less clear cases.
4. **CPM evidence is what makes or breaks a concurrent-delay claim** — the entire concurrent-delay doctrine exists only because "the concept of the critical path ... distinguishes critical work activities from noncritical ones, that opened the possibility for finding that Delay 1 was on the critical path ... while Delay 2 was not critical." A weak programme is a weak concurrent-delay case.
5. **The "apportionment" word is doing two jobs in this case law** — it can mean apportioning *fault* (rarely allowed) or apportioning *time* (the norm). Saudi tribunals typically apportion time, not fault; structure your claim to align.
