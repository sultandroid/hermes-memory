## Direct Quote
> "The two major pieces of legislation that govern the construction law industry in Saudi Arabia (the 'Kingdom') are the New Civil Transactions Law (NCTL) and the Saudi Building Code (SBC)."

> "The SBC (enacted by Royal Decree No M/43/2017 and amended by Royal Decree M/15/2019 (as amended by Royal Decree M/88/2024)) and its implementing regulations play a crucial role in regulating the construction industry and aim to streamline the execution of construction projects in Saudi Arabia."

> "In practice, the most common construction contracts used in Saudi Arabia stem from the International Federation of Consulting Engineers (FIDIC). The predominant forms are the first editions (published in 1999) of the [Red, Yellow and Silver Books]."

> "It is common for FIDIC contracts to be modified, often in favour of the employer. This redistribution of risk can be extensive. The engineer's role in the FIDIC Red Book form is often limited. The employer's approval is therefore required for various important decisions."

> "Sharing responsibility for certain risks is common practice in construction contracts in Saudi Arabia. Risks such as design changes, unforeseen site conditions and force majeure events may be shared by the parties through contractual provisions."

> "In Saudi Arabia, the scope and price for variations requested by the employer are typically determined through the contract terms, which may include provisions outlining the process for evaluating and pricing variations based on agreed rates or pricing mechanisms. The contract may also specify limitations on the scope and cost of variations to ensure that they are reasonable and within the project's budget."

## Summary (2–3 sentences)
This Chambers practice guide is the closest thing to a *current* jurisdictional operating manual for a Saudi-delivered, FIDIC-based museum project. It confirms the legal stack (NCTL 2023 + SBC 2017/2019/2024, with the Saudi Building Code Center having migrated from SASO to MOMAH in 2025), notes that FIDIC Red Book 1999 is the predominant form (heavily modified in favour of the employer), and that "the engineer's role in the FIDIC Red Book form is often limited. The employer's approval is therefore required for various important decisions." For a design manager this is the most dangerous single sentence in the entire guide: where the employer's representative — not the design consultant — holds the design-change approval pen, the consultant's design responsibility is uncoupled from the consultant's design authority. That asymmetry is the engine of design freeze disputes and the reason an EOT/cost VO can be refused even when the consultant recommended it.

## 5 Lessons Extracted
1. **Vocabulary lesson:** *New Civil Transactions Law* (NCTL — نظام المعاملات المدنية الجديد) Royal Decree M/191/2023 supersedes the old Civil Transactions Law for any contract signed after its effective date; it is the *mandatory background law* behind every bespoke design-services agreement. *Saudi Building Code* (SBC — كود البناء السعودي) is the technical compliance layer, not a contract.
2. **Evidence lesson:** The *employer's approval* is the operative approval in a modified FIDIC Red Book in Saudi Arabia — not the Engineer's. Any design change request (DCR) that the consultant recommends but the employer's representative withholds must be documented with date-stamped correspondence; that paper trail is what later proves the consultant discharged their design duty and shifted the risk to the employer.
3. **Procedural lesson:** Build the design change-control workflow around the *employer's approval* gate, not the consultant's design responsibility matrix. Three artefacts are non-negotiable: (a) the DCR form with cost/time impact; (b) the consultant's technical response within the SBC-mandated review window; (c) the employer's representative's written approval/rejection. Without (c) the design manager cannot defend either a Time for Completion claim or a defects-liability dispute.
4. **Strategic lesson:** The guide's note that "the contract may also specify limitations on the scope and cost of variations" is a flashing red light for any museum project with a long design phase — the variation cap (e.g. 10–15% of contract value) should be tested at *tender* against the museum's design-development volatility (exhibition fit-out, gallery lighting, wayfinding). If the cap is too tight, the contractor will refuse to absorb scope creep and the design will be frozen early at suboptimal resolution.
5. **The principle:** *"The engineer's role in the FIDIC Red Book form is often limited. The employer's approval is therefore required for various important decisions."* In Saudi-FIDIC, design authority sits with the *employer's representative*, not the consultant of record. A design manager who plans the design-freeze gate as if they were still in a UK-Red-Book world will be ambushed.
