## Direct Quote
> "Why EPC Projects Don't Fail in Construction — They Fail in Engineering. Most EPC leaders still believe that projects fail on site. That delays, cost overruns, and disputes are primarily construction problems. In reality, most EPC project outcomes are already decided long before construction starts. From experience, **nearly 70% of EPC issues are locked in during the engineering phase**, through:"

> "**Late or continuously changing design freeze**"

> "**Over-optimistic engineering schedules driven by commercial pressure**"

> "**Inadequate constructability and operability reviews**"

> "**Incomplete or late vendor data for long-lead equipment.**"

> "When IFC drawings are issued late, rushed, or with gaps, construction teams are left with only one option: react, workaround, and firefight. At that point, even the best construction team can only manage the consequences — not the root causes."

> "The strongest EPC projects I've been part of shared one common trait: they were engineering-led projects, not construction-led recoveries. Engineering was treated as: A decision-making function, A risk-reduction mechanism, The foundation for cost, schedule, safety, and quality."

> "👉 Engineering is not a cost center. It is the primary risk control center of an EPC project. When engineering is done right, construction becomes execution — not damage control."

## Summary (2–3 sentences)
This LinkedIn post by an EPC project-management leader is the most concise published statement of the design-freeze failure mode: "*nearly 70% of EPC issues are locked in during the engineering phase*", and the four named engineering-phase failure causes are (1) *late or continuously changing design freeze*, (2) *over-optimistic engineering schedules driven by commercial pressure*, (3) *inadequate constructability and operability reviews*, and (4) *incomplete or late vendor data for long-lead equipment*. The argument is operational, not academic: an engineering-led project treats engineering as a *decision-making function* and a *risk-reduction mechanism*; a construction-led recovery treats engineering as a paperwork function. For a museum Tech Office the translation is the same: a project that does not have a *hard* design freeze at RIBA Stage 4 will spend its construction phase in continuous design churn, with the 70% dispute risk converted into actual variation and claim cost.

## 5 Lessons Extracted
1. **Vocabulary lesson:** *Design freeze* (تجميد التصميم) in EPC terms is the *date-certain milestone* at which the design is locked for construction, after which any change is a *formal* variation (cost + time impact) rather than an *informal* design update. In practice, design freeze is a *spectrum* (concept freeze, basic-engineering freeze, detailed-engineering freeze) not a single event — and the most common failure is to *announce* a freeze that the design team then *unfreezes* under commercial pressure.
2. **Evidence lesson:** The "70% locked in during engineering" claim is supported by Syed's practical experience, by the HKA CRUX Saudi data (Case 03, 97.2% EOT average), by the CII constructability research (Case 06, late-input constructability has no measurable benefit), and by the FIDIC Cl. 13.1 right-to-vary framework. The convergent evidence is that engineering quality is the *single* largest controllable determinant of construction outcomes.
3. **Procedural lesson:** A design freeze gate requires four artefacts: (a) a *Design Freeze Certificate* signed by the consultant and the employer's representative, listing the drawings/models under freeze by revision number; (b) a *Freeze Exception Register* that lists the items deliberately excluded from the freeze (e.g. long-lead vendor data still pending); (c) a *Post-Freeze Change Procedure* with named approver and SLA; (d) a *Constructability Review Log* of items closed before freeze. Without (a)–(d), "design freeze" is a wish, not a gate.
4. **Strategic lesson:** Syed's four engineering failure modes map directly to four management interventions: (1) hold the freeze even under commercial pressure (governance), (2) build engineering schedule from the bottom up, not the top down (planning), (3) require CII-style constructability reviews at every RIBA stage (Case 06), (4) treat vendor data as a *design input*, not a *procurement output* (vendor engagement). A Tech Office that owns all four interventions is a *design management* office, not a *design administration* office.
5. **The principle:** *"Engineering is not a cost center. It is the primary risk control center of an EPC project."* Whether the project is an EPC refinery or a museum, the engineering/design phase is where 70% of the dispute risk is created or eliminated. The Tech Office that budgets and staffs accordingly is the one that prevents disputes; the Tech Office that treats design as a pre-construction chore is the one that signs the variation orders.

## Open Questions for Follow-up
- What is the empirical "70%" based on? Syed cites "experience" rather than a published dataset. HKA CRUX (Case 03) gives a parallel figure (97.2% EOT average) but does not attribute the breakdown to engineering vs construction causes. A combined dataset would be powerful.
- For a museum project specifically, is "design freeze" achievable at the same point as an EPC plant? A museum's fit-out (exhibitions, galleries) is often designed *after* the base build is frozen, which means the freeze is for the shell only. How is the *shell freeze vs fit-out freeze* boundary managed?
- Does the Aseer Museum (or comparable Saudi giga-museum) have a *named engineering-freeze authority* — i.e. a single individual with the power to refuse a freeze-breaking change? Without one, the freeze is a fiction.
