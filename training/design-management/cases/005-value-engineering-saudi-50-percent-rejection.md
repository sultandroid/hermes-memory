## Direct Quote
> "Several developing countries around the globe have launched their future visions, which include concrete strategic plans that involve many kinds of mega-construction projects. One common goal across these visions is to increase the efficiency of construction projects' huge expenditures using recent sustainable, practical engineering tools, such as the value engineering (VE) approach. Although the VE approach has been around for a few years now, some barriers prevent the popularity of its application in different construction projects."

> "Value engineering (VE) is a methodology that analyzes value to obtain the required functionality level at the lowest cost without compromising the quality, reliability, services, and performance [1]. It was developed in the U.S. in 1947 after the Second World War. Due to the effectiveness of the VE approach in terms of cost savings, it is applied in different industries, one of which is the construction industry."

> "Lastly, a study titled *The Success Factors for Implementing VE Proposals in Saudi Arabia* focused mainly on success factors that will assist in obtaining the decision-maker's approval of the proposed VE study during the implementation meeting [21]. It was found that **50% of VE proposals were rejected for two main reasons: inappropriate timing for submitting the VE proposal and poor quality of the VE proposal.** The study was mainly focused on the phase after completing the VE workshop and before implementation."

## Summary (2–3 sentences)
The MDPI systematic review draws together the global and Saudi-specific value-engineering literature and surfaces a striking Saudi-specific finding: **50% of VE proposals are rejected**, and they are rejected for *two reasons only* — inappropriate timing and poor proposal quality. This is critical for the design manager because the same review notes that mega-project expenditure under Saudi Vision 2030 is precisely the use case where VE should be most valuable, yet the *process* by which VE is run is what kills it. The design manager's role is not to "encourage VE" (every Saudi client does that) but to design the *VE gate* so that proposals arrive at the right RIBA stage and in the right form — too early is wasted effort, too late is a variation instruction, never is lost savings.

## 5 Lessons Extracted
1. **Vocabulary lesson:** *Value engineering* (هندسة القيمة) is a *systematic function-analysis* exercise, not a cost-cutting exercise; the SAVE International definition is "the systematic application of recognised techniques which identify the function of a product or service, establish a monetary value for that function, and provide the necessary function reliably at the lowest overall cost." Note: "lowest *cost to deliver the function*", not "lowest cost to build the artefact."
2. **Evidence lesson:** The Saudi VE literature has measured two specific failure modes — *inappropriate timing* and *poor quality of the VE proposal*. Both are observable in the project record: timing is a date-stamp comparison between VE submission and the RIBA Stage 4 (Technical Design) gate; quality is a checklist test against the SAVE International Job Plan (Information, Function Analysis, Creative, Evaluation, Development, Recommendation, Implementation).
3. **Procedural lesson:** Build the VE process *into* the design programme with explicit submission windows — typically (a) Function Analysis at RIBA Stage 2 (Concept Design), (b) Creative Speculative at Stage 3 (Spatial Coordination), (c) VE Proposals at Stage 4 (Technical Design) *before* design freeze. VE proposals that arrive after Stage 4 are *de facto* Variations under FIDIC Cl. 13 and lose their cost-share benefit to the contractor.
4. **Strategic lesson:** The reward/risk asymmetry for the *proposer* of a VE idea is severe — under FIDIC Red/Yellow Book Cl. 13.2, the savings are *shared* (typically 50/50) but the *risk* of a failed VE (loss of function, programme slip, dispute) is borne by the contractor unless the contract says otherwise. For a museum with a long design phase and a custom fit-out, this asymmetry is the reason contractors refuse to submit VE — they do not get paid to take the risk. The design manager must *price the risk* into the VE invitation to get good proposals.
5. **The principle:** *"50% of VE proposals were rejected for two main reasons: inappropriate timing for submitting the VE proposal and poor quality of the VE proposal."* A 50% rejection rate is a process problem, not a concept problem. The Tech Office that measures VE *acceptance rate* and feeds the data back to the design team is the one that closes the gap.

## Open Questions for Follow-up
- What is the VE *acceptance* rate on Saudi giga-projects specifically (NEOM, Diriyah, Red Sea, Qiddiya) vs the 50% figure in the academic literature? The mega-projects are the high-stakes test.
- How does VE interact with the SBC compliance gate? A VE proposal that saves cost but changes a fire-rated assembly may fail SBC; who arbitrates that trade-off?
- Does the consultant's PI cover a VE that the consultant *proposes*? Most PI policies exclude design changes that were not part of the original brief — a VE by the consultant that fails is uninsured unless the policy is endorsed.
