# Contract Administration — Top Lessons

> **Curated wisdom** extracted from articles in `cases/`. The 5-lesson extraction method is in `../READING_GUIDE.md`.

## From Articles 001 (Symms) + 011 (Strata) — FIDIC 20.1 Notice Trap

1. **"Condition precedent" is a hard bar in English law, but GCC civil codes preserve escape routes.**
   If the clause says "shall not be entitled" *and* names a fixed period (28 days), common-law courts enforce it as a hard bar. But UAE Civil Code Arts. 106, 243, 265, 318, 319 and Qatari Arts. 171, 172, 220 give grounds to refuse enforcement where strict time-bar enforcement would be "unlawful exercise of rights" or "unjust enrichment." **Drafting consequence:** when pleading a missed notice in GCC, always plead the civil-code escape routes as separate grounds; courts won't raise them sua sponte.

2. **The 28-day clock starts on awareness, not on impact.**
   The trigger is "the Contractor became aware (or should have become aware) of the event or circumstance." Site diaries, monthly reports noting the first date of impact are the linchpin when the other side argues you knew earlier.

3. **The 28-day notice is the #1 failure mode on Vision 2030 mega-projects.**
   Per Strata (2024): 28-day notice compliance is consistently the highest-failure item on Saudi mega-projects. **Operational rule:** if a delay is even suspected, issue the notice by day 14; you can supplement the particulars later.

## From Articles 002 (El Haggan) + 003 (UAE 880/883) + 013 (22-year Abu Dhabi case) — Limitation Periods

4. **Contractual time bars and statutory limitation periods run in parallel.**
   Egyptian 5/15-year limitation (El Haggan) and UAE Art. 880/883 (3 years from defect discovery) coexist with the FIDIC 20.1 contractual bar. **Losing one claim window can extinguish both the contractual and the statutory remedy.**

5. **Article 883 (UAE) 3-year bar runs from defect *discovery*, not from handover.**
   A 22-year Abu Dhabi case (Federal Supreme Court 319 & 320/2015) ended on this bar. **Joinder does not reset the clock** — adding a party later doesn't buy you a fresh limitation period.

6. **Article 880 (UAE) creates 10-year decennial liability from handover.**
   Different from the 3-year Art. 883 bar; both can apply to the same defect. The decennial period is the absolute outer limit for structural liability.

## From Articles 005 (Abu Dhabi Cassation) + 006 (Dubai Cassation 41/2012) — Termination vs Delay Damages

7. **Termination extinguishes the contractual delay-damages clause.**
   Abu Dhabi Court of Cassation 424 & 483/2015: once the contract is terminated, the contractual delay-damages regime ends; the employer must claim in tort. **Drafting consequence:** if you're the contractor facing termination, push for the contractual delay-damages route (cap, formula, predefined LDs); if you're the employer, terminate cleanly and switch to a tort claim.

8. **Failure to perform a reciprocal obligation on time = material breach.**
   Dubai Court of Cassation 41/2012: Article 247 entitles termination where one party fails to perform a reciprocal obligation on time. The breach must be material, not trivial.

## From Articles 007 (O'Leary) + 008 (Concurrency) — EOT Evidence

9. **The as-built programme cross-checked against contemporaneous records is the linchpin EOT document.**
   Site diaries, monthly reports, weather logs, plant returns, RFI logs — all must align with the as-built programme. Inconsistencies between the programme and the records kill the EOT.

10. **The *Malmaison* approach is most likely applied in UAE concurrency cases.**
    Employer-risk event → EOT awarded. Overlap between employer-risk and contractor-risk → no time-related cost (EOT yes, money no). **Drafting consequence:** if you are the contractor, isolate each delay event by cause and avoid pleading a "global" delay; if you are the employer, demand the contractor isolate causes.

## From Article 004 (FIDIC 2017 Yellow Book) — Cl. 3.7 Engineer's Determination

11. **The engineer under FIDIC 2017 Cl. 3.7 must act *neutrally* (not as the employer's agent).**
    The 1999 Red Book engineer was the employer's agent. 2017 Yellow Book's Cl. 3.7 introduces fair determination by a neutral engineer — a material change.

12. **The 28-day Notice of Dissatisfaction (NOD) is the only way to refuse a Cl. 3.7 determination.**
    Miss the NOD → the engineer's determination becomes binding. **Operational rule:** on receipt of any Cl. 3.7 determination, serve the NOD within 14 days if there's any disagreement; you can refine the grounds later.

## From Article 009 (Ede) + 010 (HKA) — Force Majeure and Provisional Sums

13. **"Prevented" in force majeure requires physical or legal impossibility, not commercial difficulty.**
    Per *Tennants v Wilson* [1917] AC 495 (still cited in FIDIC 2017 commentary): "prevented" is high bar. Increased cost, market disruption, or financial difficulty do not qualify. **Drafting consequence:** if your FM clause uses "prevented," don't claim FM for cost escalation.

14. **The FIDIC 1999 RB has a genuine Provisional Sum time lacuna.**
    HKA (2017): PS work triggers Cl. 13 (variations) and Cl. 8.4(a) extension; the contractor's best argument is the *interim* measurement under Cl. 14.3, not waiting for the final PS settlement.

## From Article 012 (Stonehaven) — Scope Creep vs Variation

15. **Variation ≠ scope creep; the difference is change-control governance.**
    A variation is a documented change to scope, priced and approved via the change-control process. Scope creep is unauthorized addition of work without paperwork. **The legal consequence is severe:** a variation is paid; scope creep may be paid only on quantum meruit (if at all).
