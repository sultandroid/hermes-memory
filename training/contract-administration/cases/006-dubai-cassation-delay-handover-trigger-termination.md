# Case 06 — Dubai Court of Cassation: Delay in Handover Triggers Termination (Dubai Court 41/2012)

## Citation
- **Title:** "Dubai Courts hold that delay in handover can trigger termination"
- **Author:** Rami Abdellatif (and Marc Sheridan) — Al Tamimi & Company, Dubai
- **Year:** March 2015
- **URL:** https://www.tamimi.com/law-update/march-8/articles/dubai-courts-hold-that-delay-in-handover-can-trigger-termination/

## Quick Facts
- **Forum:** Dubai Court of Cassation
- **Project type:** Off-plan villa sale (developer vs. purchaser) — but the reasoning directly imports civil-code handover principles applicable to construction contracts
- **Contract value:** AED 4m villa; ordered reimbursement AED 3,477,701 + 9% interest
- **Outcome for purchaser:** Won at First Instance, lost at Appeal, **won at Cassation**

## Theme Mapped
**2. Extension of Time / delay** + downstream termination rights + civil-code Articles 246/247

## Direct Quote
> "The Court found that a default by the developer in the handover of the unit would entitle the purchaser to terminate the contract. In response to the developer's argument that the purchaser did not pay the whole purchase price, the Court stated that the purchaser may withhold payment of the balance of the purchase price even if the payment is due if there is good reason to suspect that the seller will not perform his obligation to deliver the thing sold. The Court relied on Article 247 of the Civil Code which states: 'In contracts which are binding on the parties, the performance of reciprocal obligations is tied to mutual commitments. Either party may withhold performance of an obligation if the other party fails to perform his own reciprocal obligation on time.'"

> "The Court also stated that a developer's dispute with governmental entities does not excuse the developer from fulfilling its contractual duties. The right of a purchaser to withhold any payment and seek termination of the contract is established on the next day following the handover date."

## Summary
A purchaser contracted in 2006 to buy an off-plan villa for AED 4m, with handover in December 2007. In September 2007 the developer unilaterally pushed the delivery to June 2008; on that missed date, the purchaser sued for termination and refund. The Court of First Instance granted the relief (AED 3.47m + 9% interest). The Court of Appeal reversed, accepting an expert's view that the villa was 70% complete and the breach was not "material." The Cassation Court reversed the Appeal and reinstated the First Instance judgment, holding that the failure to deliver on the agreed handover date *is itself* a material breach that triggers the purchaser's right to terminate — and that Article 247 of the UAE Civil Code allows the purchaser to withhold any further instalments as soon as the developer fails to perform its reciprocal obligation. The developer's argument that it was delayed by disputes with government entities was rejected as irrelevant.

## 5 Lessons Extracted
1. **Vocabulary lesson:** "Material breach" is not the test the Cassation applied — the test is *failure to perform a reciprocal obligation on time* under Article 247. The "materiality" framing the Appeal Court used is incorrect.
2. **Evidence lesson:** The developer's own letter to the purchaser (September 2007, acknowledging the new delivery date) was the smoking gun — it conceded the delay. Every correspondence from a developer or contractor that acknowledges a new programme date is a potential admission of breach.
3. **Procedural lesson:** The Appeal Court's reasoning was internally contradictory (it found a breach but called it not material); the Cassation attacked the contradiction rather than re-trying the facts. On appeal, attack the lower court's logic chain, not its findings of fact.
4. **Strategic lesson:** For a developer/contractor: *never write a letter agreeing to a new completion date* unless the EOT clause is explicitly invoked in the same letter. For a purchaser/employer: *demand such a letter* — it locks in the breach.
5. **The principle:** *"A developer's dispute with governmental entities does not excuse contractual performance."* Force majeure / exceptional-circumstances arguments (Case 09) are limited to events that are *beyond* the party's control and *unforeseeable* — ordinary permitting delays are foreseeable and on the developer's risk.

## Open Questions for Follow-up
- How would the Cassation Court have ruled if the developer had issued a formal EOT under a FIDIC Cl. 20.1 / 8.4 notice — does the contractual EOT mechanism displace the Article 247 termination right?
- Does Saudi law (which lacks an identical Article 247) follow the same outcome under general contract-law principles?
