## Theme Mapped
**6. Conditions Precedent in FIDIC Cl. 20.1** + **3. Time-bar (سقوط الحق) cases** — when does the 28-day notice clock start, and can it be cured?

## Direct Quote
> "If the Contractor fails to give notice of a claim within such period of 28 days the Time for Completion shall not be extended, the Contractor shall not be entitled to additional payment, and the Employer shall be discharged from all liability in connection with the claim…."

> "Contractual terms requiring a contractor to give prompt notice of delay serve a valuable purpose; such notice enables matters to be investigated while they are still current. Furthermore, such notice sometimes gives the employer the opportunity to withdraw instructions when the financial consequences become apparent." — *Multiplex Construction v Honeywell Control Systems*, cited approvingly.

## Summary (2–3 sentences)
Symms walks through FIDIC 1999 Red Book Sub-Clause 20.1's "harsh and unambiguous" 28-day condition-precedent language and the common-law test from *Bremer Handelgesellschaft v Vanden Avenne Izegem* (clear time + clear consequence = enforceable condition precedent). He then contrasts that with the more lenient approach available in the UAE Civil Code (Articles 106, 243, 265, 318, 319) and Qatari law (Articles 171, 172, 220), which give courts grounds to refuse enforcement where strict time-bar enforcement would be "unlawful exercise of rights" or "unjust enrichment." The article is a must-read GCC counterweight to the common-law "you blew the notice, you lose" reflex.

## 5 Lessons Extracted
1. **Vocabulary lesson:** "Condition precedent" (في الشرط المعلق / شرط سابق) is the operative label — if the clause says "shall not be entitled" *and* names a fixed period, common-law courts treat it as a hard bar. Drafters use the word "discharged" deliberately.
2. **Evidence lesson:** The trigger for the 28-day clock is "the Contractor became aware (or should have become aware) of the event or circumstance" — contemporaneous awareness logs (e.g., site diaries, monthly reports noting the first date of impact) are the linchpin when the other side argues you knew earlier.
3. **Procedural lesson:** GCC civil codes preserve three independent escape routes — (a) Article 106 UAE — unlawful exercise of rights (disproportionate harm), (b) Articles 318–319 UAE — unjust enrichment, (c) the "penalty" doctrine allowing courts to read down a clause that operates as a punishment rather than a genuine pre-estimate of loss. Each must be pleaded as a separate ground; courts will not raise them *sua sponte*.
4. **Strategic lesson:** A contractor who missed the 28-day notice should still *issue the notice late* with a covering letter pleading each civil-code escape route and quantifying actual loss — GCC courts have shown a willingness to forgive technical breaches where the employer suffered no real prejudice from the delay.
5. **The principle:** *"Notice provisions serve a valuable purpose only insofar as they enable investigation while facts are current."* The legitimate purpose of the 28-day bar is evidentiary, not punitive — GCC courts may refuse to enforce a clause that has been weaponised into a pure technicality.
