## Theme Mapped
**2. Extension of Time (EOT) — what evidence wins an EOT**

## Direct Quote
> "When pursuing delay claims proof will be needed to demonstrate that: (i) the event actually took place; (ii) the event is catered for within the contract; (iii) the relevant notices have been issued; (iv) the event had a delaying effect on the completion date; and (v) a loss was suffered as a result of this delay."

> "A simple definition of an as-built programme may be stated as: 'a record of when the various project works were actually undertaken.' A fuller definition may be stated as: '…the final [programme] to be completed. It will evolve through the course of the project as activities are started, progressed and completed as the work is executed. The last update should complete the as-built [programme] as a high-density record of the sequence in which the works were actually constructed, the resources actually used and the productivity actually achieved.'"

> "If the as-built programme is not cross-checked against contemporaneous records then it may not be truly accurate. This will mean that the subsequent research and assessment of delays may be found to be of little use, and this may only come to light either following a review by the other party's delay expert, or worse still, during cross examination by opposing counsel at an evidentiary hearing, and after the client has incurred significant costs."

## Summary
O'Leary identifies the five cumulative elements an EOT claim must prove and argues that the as-built programme — built bottom-up from the contractor's monthly progress reports and cross-checked against the EDMS-archived contemporaneous records (delivery notes, plant movement logs, weather logs, RFI turnaround times, EI issue dates) — is the single most important evidentiary document. Without a cross-checked as-built, even a meritorious claim will collapse under the other side's delay expert or in cross-examination. The article warns that monthly progress reports themselves cannot be trusted uncritically (contractors routinely overstate progress in those reports) and that the as-built must be *constructed* from primary contemporaneous documents, not just *described* in the monthly reports.

## 5 Lessons Extracted
1. **Vocabulary lesson:** *As-built programme* ≠ *updated programme*. The as-built is the final historical record; the updated programme is a forward-looking forecast. The Arabic distinction (البرنامج الفعلي / البرنامج المحدث) maps the same way.
2. **Evidence lesson:** The four primary source documents are monthly progress reports, delivery notes, plant-equipment movement logs, and the EDMS-archived RFIs / EIs / weather logs. Each activity on the as-built must be verifiable in at least one of these.
3. **Procedural lesson:** Build the as-built *during* the project, not after. Witness evidence is a poor substitute for documents; tribunals give far more weight to records created at the time events occurred.
4. **Strategic lesson:** On a Saudi Vision 2030 mega-project with thousands of activities, the as-built is best built activity-by-activity from the contemporaneous records into a project-management tool (P6 / Primavera) with the original baseline programme as the skeleton — not retrospectively "reverse-engineered" from memory.
5. **The principle:** *"Proof of cause and effect is impossible without a record of effect (the as-built) that has been cross-checked against the record of cause (the contemporaneous documents)."* A delay expert whose as-built is not supported by primary documents will be impeached.
