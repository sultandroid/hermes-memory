## Direct Quote
> "Clause 19.1 of the FIDIC Yellow and Silver Books defines force majeure as: 'an exceptional event or circumstance: which is beyond a Party's control, which such Party could not have reasonably provided against before entering into the Contract, which, having arisen, such Party could not reasonably have avoided or overcome, and which is not substantially attributable to the other Party.'"

> "Clause 19.2 refers to a party being 'prevented', not to it being 'hindered' or 'delayed' or 'subjected to increased cost'. In Benjamin's Sale of Goods (8th Ed 2011), the leading text on these matters, it was explained (at para 8-092) that '[a party which] seeks to invoke the protection of a clause which states that he is to be relieved of liability if he is "prevented" from carrying out his obligations under the contract or "unable" to do so, he must show that performance has become physically or legally impossible, and not merely more difficult or unprofitable'. This was cited with approval in Dunavant Enterprises Inc v Olympia Spinning & Weaving Mills [2011] EWHC 2028."

> "In the leading case of Tennants (Lancashire) Ltd v CS Wilson & Co [1917] AC 495 (at p510), Lord Loreburn observed that 'the argument that a man can be excused from performance of his contract when it becomes "commercially impossible" seems to me to be a dangerous contention which ought not to be admitted unless the parties plainly contracted to that effect.'"

## Summary
Ede walks through FIDIC Yellow/Silver Book Cl. 19, applied to the 2014 ISIS insurgency in Iraq. He confirms that war, terrorism and invasion are listed events in Cl. 19.1's non-exhaustive list and that contractors in the relevant Iraqi governorates will likely meet the "beyond control" and "not reasonably provided against" prongs. The harder question is Cl. 19.2's "prevented" wording — under English law (the most common governing law for major Iraqi projects), this requires *physical or legal impossibility*, not mere commercial difficulty. The article warns that contractors who simply argue "we couldn't work as efficiently" or "our insurance costs went up" will fail; they must show that performance of specific contractual obligations became impossible. The Cl. 19.6 termination trigger (84 continuous days or 140 aggregate days of prevented performance) is also flagged.

## 5 Lessons Extracted
1. **Vocabulary lesson:** "Prevented" (ممنوع / محال) is the test under FIDIC 19.2; "delayed" (مؤجل) or "hindered" (معاق) is not. The English-law distinction is sharp and routinely lost in Arabic pleadings.
2. **Evidence lesson:** The contractor must document which *specific* obligations it could not perform — not just that conditions were difficult. Critical-path activities that were physically impossible to execute should be itemised, with security advisor reports and government-issued travel advisories.
3. **Procedural lesson:** Cl. 19.2's 14-day notice is a *separate* time bar from Cl. 20.1's 28-day claim notice. A contractor who relies on FM must comply with *both* or lose the claim under the FIDIC procedure.
4. **Strategic lesson:** Force majeure is *not* a variation. A contractor who has a legitimate FM claim but a contractually-weak variation claim should not pitch it as a Cl. 13 variation — the Cl. 19 FM route preserves different remedies (Cl. 19.4: EOT *and* cost, vs. Cl. 13: EOT and cost on valuation).
5. **The principle:** *"Force majeure excuses non-performance when performance has become physically or legally impossible — not when it has become merely more difficult or unprofitable."* The bar is set deliberately high to prevent commercial hardship from being repackaged as legal impossibility.
