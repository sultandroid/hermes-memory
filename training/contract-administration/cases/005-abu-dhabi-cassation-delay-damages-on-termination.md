## Quick Facts
- **Forum:** Abu Dhabi Court of Cassation
- **Doc type:** Reported court judgment summarised by counsel
- **Project:** FIDIC 1999 Red Book contract for a mall + hotel, AED 418m (amended to AED 355m); AED 258m of work done
- **Outcome for employer:** **Lost the contractual delay-damages claim** — but preserved a parallel tortious-liability claim. The Court of Cassation held that delay damages under the contract are extinguished by termination; the employer's only remedy is tort (general damages).

## Direct Quote
> "In a case in which Al Tamimi & Company represented the contractor, the Abu Dhabi Court of Cassation held, in Appeal Case 424 & 483 of 2015, that if the employer terminates the contractor before the completion of the works, the employer is not entitled to sue for the Delay Damages stated in the contract. However, the Cassation Court highlighted that the employer, in such circumstances, is entitled to prove and claim for any damage that may have resulted from the contractor's delay (i.e. claim damages in tort)."

> "The contractor argued that if the original obligation (i.e. the obligation to execute the project) is terminated, then the other obligations set out in the said contract, including the Delay Damages clause, are also terminated and cannot be applied anymore. Accordingly, the legal basis for claiming compensation, if any, can only be based on tortious liability."

## Summary
In a FIDIC 1999 Red Book project (AED 418m mall + hotel), the employer terminated the contractor after the contractor had completed AED 258m of work. The contractor sued for its unpaid balance; the employer counterclaimed for the contractually-stipulated delay damages (approximately AED 41m per the Court-appointed experts). The Abu Dhabi Court of First Instance and Court of Appeal both found the contractor's principal claim premature, and the Court of Cassation (Cases 424 & 483 of 2015) added a critical holding: *when the employer terminates the contract, the contractual delay-damages clause falls with it.* The employer must instead prove and plead its losses in tort — losing the convenience of the liquidated sum and the burden-shifting that a properly-drafted delay-damages clause provides. The article emphasises that this is a UAE-specific civil-code consequence: the contractual clause is treated as an accessory obligation that dies with the principal obligation.

## 5 Lessons Extracted
1. **Vocabulary lesson:** The case turns on a civil-code classification: contractual delay damages are an *accessory obligation* (التزام تبعي) — they die with the principal obligation to complete the works. This is conceptually different from English-law treatment of LDs as a debt.
2. **Evidence lesson:** The Court-appointed engineering experts ("experts" appointed by the Court under UAE procedure) produced a dissenting opinion on whether delay damages should even be deducted. Expert reports are decisive — appoint your own delay expert early and engage with the Court-appointed expert's working hypotheses.
3. **Procedural lesson:** The employer's failure to challenge the Appeal Court's *rejection* of its counterclaim at the Court of Cassation was fatal — the Cassation only heard the contractor's appeal. Always cross-appeal on every adverse finding.
4. **Strategic lesson:** For a contractor: *welcome termination*. For an employer: *do not terminate if you can avoid it* — you will lose the LDs and have to re-prove damages in tort. Prefer to keep the contract alive, levy LDs, and complete via the contractor.
5. **The principle:** *"Termination kills the delay-damages clause."* A UAE employer's most consequential contract-administration decision is whether to terminate; once terminated, the contractually-bargained-for liquidated-sum remedy is replaced by an open-ended tort claim that the employer must prove element by element.
