## Direct Quote
> "A definition of the latter [concurrent delay], which is often used by English lawyers, is: 'A period of project overrun which is caused by two or more effective causes of delay which are of equal causative potency.'"

> "Henry Boot v Malmaison [1999] 70 ConLR 32 – It was agreed between the parties in this case that if there are two concurrent causes of delay, one of which is a relevant event, then the contractor is entitled to an extension of time for the delay caused by the relevant event, provided it can be shown to have caused a critical delay."

> "Balfour Beatty v Chestermount [1993] 62 BLR 1 – …the contractor was entitled to an extension of time attributable to a (post-completion) variation/instruction, but that the period of the extension should only be 'dotted-on' to the original or extended completion date (colloquially known as the 'dot-on' or 'net' approach). However, and crucially, the relevant event must still be shown to cause a critical delay. It is not enough that a relevant event occurred; a causal link between cause and effect must still be established."

## Summary
This article maps the English common-law authorities on concurrent delay (Trollope & Colls, Percy Bilton, SMK Cabinets, H Fairweather, Balfour Beatty, Henry Boot) and explains that, while not formally binding in the UAE, the *Malmaison* approach is likely to be applied by common-law-trained arbitrators in DIAC-seated proceedings. Under *Malmaison*, if two delays overlap and one is an employer-risk "relevant event" and the other is contractor-risk, the contractor is entitled to an EOT (no time penalty) but the contractor is *not* entitled to time-related cost compensation for the overlap period. The article also flags the *Balfour Beatty v Chestermount* "dot-on" approach for post-completion variation events.

## 5 Lessons Extracted
1. **Vocabulary lesson:** *Concurrent delay* (تأخير متزامن) is two or more effective causes of *equal causative potency* — different from *sequential delay* (where one cause finishes before the next begins). Most GCC claims are mislabelled "concurrent" when they are actually sequential.
2. **Evidence lesson:** The as-built programme (Case 07) is the only document that can demonstrate *which path on the critical path was actually driving the completion date at the moment of each delay event.* Without it, "concurrency" is a lawyer's argument, not a fact.
3. **Procedural lesson:** Even under the most contractor-favourable English approach (*Malmaison*), the contractor must still prove a *causal* link to a critical path. The mere occurrence of a relevant event on a non-critical activity does not earn an EOT.
4. **Strategic lesson:** When two events run in parallel, present the analysis in *layers* — first, what was the critical path; second, which event affected the critical path; third, was the contractor-risk event on the *same* activity as the employer-risk event. The third layer is where concurrency is won or lost.
5. **The principle:** *"An EOT is for the critical delay caused by a relevant event; cost (and LD protection) is a separate question that depends on whether the contractor's own delay was on the *same* critical activity."* Don't conflate the two analyses.
